# Chapter 1
## The Accession

Lucas Mercer was halfway through explaining why the contractor did not need the more expensive machine when the world blinked. One second he was standing beside a row of parked lifts in a sun-baked equipment yard, one hand on a steel guardrail hot enough to punish anyone who forgot it was metal; the next, every shadow in the yard vanished.

Lucas stopped talking, and so did the man across from him. For one impossible second the afternoon sun seemed to exist without direction, light coming from everywhere and nowhere at once. The yard flattened into something painted: white service trucks lost their reflections, blue scissor lifts stopped throwing long bars of shade across the concrete, and even the dust hanging between them looked frozen. Then every engine died. The idling diesel lift beside Lucas coughed once and went silent, a delivery truck rolling toward the front gate coasted another ten feet before the driver hit the brakes, and somewhere beyond the chain-link fence the traffic noise collapsed into a spreading hush.

Lucas stared at the truck. “Did we lose power?” the contractor asked. Lucas looked at him. The man’s name was Dean; Lucas had spent most of the last forty minutes talking him out of the larger unit his superintendent had requested and into two nineteen-foot electric scissors, the same conversation he had a dozen times a week. Now his phone was black. He tapped the screen, held the power button, and got nothing. Dean pulled his own phone. “Mine too.”

A mechanic came out of the shop, wiping his hands on a rag. “Hey. Anybody got power?” “No,” someone shouted from the office. “That makes no sense,” the mechanic said. Lucas turned toward the road. Cars had stopped at odd angles, a pickup sat dead in the middle of a turn lane, and people were getting out; one woman stood beside a sedan with both hands raised, demanding an answer from nobody in particular. A plane moved across the sky without sound, looking too smooth and too distant, until it flickered and simply ceased to exist.

“Did you see that?” Dean whispered. Lucas did not answer. A line of translucent white text appeared across his vision, perfectly sharp no matter which way he looked.

WORLD RECOGNITION COMPLETE.

He jerked backward hard enough to hit the lift behind him. Dean swore. All around the yard people reacted at once—someone shouted, another person dropped to a crouch, the mechanic slapped both hands over his eyes—while the text stayed.

PLANETARY DESIGNATION: EARTH.
SAPIENT POPULATION CONFIRMED.
SYSTEM COMPATIBILITY CONFIRMED.

Lucas read each line twice before a final message appeared.

ACCESSION COMMENCING.

The concrete disappeared under his feet. One instant Lucas stood in an equipment yard; the next he stood knee-deep in grass under a sky the wrong color. The grass was green, but the trees rose in thick clusters around a broad clearing, bark dark gray and leaves a deep green edged with silver. Some stood thirty feet tall while others towered high enough that he had to crane his neck to see their crowns. Above them stretched a pale blue sky crossed by two enormous cloud bands that arched from one horizon to the other like white rivers.

Lucas turned slowly. People were everywhere—dozens, hundreds, maybe more beyond the trees. Some stood alone, others had arrived close enough to bump shoulders; one man had appeared sitting and was now sprawled in the grass, a woman in blue scrubs stared at the forest with her mouth open, and someone else was screaming a name over and over. “Emma!” Lucas checked himself automatically: jeans, work boots, polo shirt, belt, wallet, keys. His phone was still in his hand, still dead. He shoved it into his pocket.

A stocky man twenty feet away spun in a circle. “Where are we?” Nobody answered. Lucas crouched and touched the grass; it was real, cooler than the yard had been, slightly damp. He pinched one blade between his fingers and pulled, and it tore with ordinary resistance.

A new pane of text appeared.

TRIAL ASSIGNMENT COMPLETE.
REALM: 7,341,882
POPULATION: 50,000
ENTRY STATUS: SUCCESSFUL

Primary Directive:
SURVIVE.

Secondary Directive:
LEARN.

Tertiary Directive:
PROGRESS.

No one spoke for several heartbeats. Then the clearing detonated into noise. “What the hell is this?” “Where are my kids?” “Fifty thousand?” “Is this VR?” “My wife was standing right next to me!” “Jesus Christ.” “Everybody calm down!”

That last one came from a broad-shouldered man in a gray button-down, late thirties or early forties, with the tone of someone used to being obeyed; at least a few people actually stopped yelling long enough to look at him. Lucas’s attention had already snagged on something else: a faint sensation at the edge of thought, more like knowing there was a door behind him without having to turn around. He focused on it and a translucent menu appeared.

STATUS
PROGRESSION
SKILLS
TITLES
ACHIEVEMENTS
NOTIFICATIONS

Someone nearby whispered, “Menu.” Lucas thought STATUS and the menu changed.

LUCAS MERCER
Race: Human — Lv. 1
Class: None
Profession: None
Rank: F

Strength: 10
Agility: 10
Vitality: 10
Endurance: 10
Perception: 10
Intelligence: 10
Wisdom: 10
Willpower: 10

Available Attribute Points: 0

He focused on Intelligence and thought Explain. A small description appeared beneath the attribute.

Intelligence:
Contributes to mana capacity, magical complexity, and cognitive processing associated with System-recognized abilities.

He checked Strength next.

Strength:
Represents physical force generation and related bodily capability. Effective output is influenced by Race, Rank, Skills, technique, condition, and other factors.

He closed the status screen. The clearing had changed while he read; people were forming knots, some families had arrived together, others wandered through the crowd asking for names. A young guy in khaki shorts held his phone above his head. “It’s dead,” Lucas said. The guy looked at him. “What?” “The phone.” “I know. I’m trying—” “It was dead before we came here. So were the vehicles. I don’t think signal is the issue.” The guy lowered it slowly. “Right.”

Lucas estimated maybe eight hundred people in the immediate clearing, though trees blocked sightlines and movement made counting useless. If the System’s number was accurate, forty-nine thousand more people were somewhere in this realm. The woman in scrubs moved past him, around thirty, dark hair tied back, hospital badge still clipped to her waistband. “Has anyone seen a boy about ten?” a frantic woman asked her. The woman in scrubs shook her head. “No, I’m sorry.” “But they said fifty thousand. He could be here.” “He could.” Her voice stayed steady. “What’s his name?” “Tyler.” “Okay. Tyler. What was he wearing?” The mother started describing him.

The gray-shirted man was organizing. “We need to know what we have,” he said. “Water. Medical supplies. Tools. Anyone injured from arrival?” “I’m a nurse,” the woman in scrubs called. The man pointed toward an open patch of ground. “Can you set up over there? Anyone with medical experience, go with her.” She hesitated, then three people nearby raised their hands and practical necessity won. “Fine,” she said.

“Name’s Grant Hale,” the man said loudly. “If you can help organize, come here. We need headcounts by group. Nobody goes into those woods alone.” A younger man wearing a faded outdoor company T-shirt laughed once. “Yeah,” he said. “That last part seems pretty reasonable.” Lucas looked at him—compact backpack, hiking shoes, the lean build of someone who spent time outside on purpose. The man noticed and said, “Owen.” “Lucas.” Owen nodded toward the forest. “Those aren’t any trees I know.” “I was hoping you were about to tell me they were decorative.” “Could be.” “Excellent. Feeling better already.” Owen snorted.

Lucas walked toward the edge of the clearing. Grant’s voice followed him. “I said nobody goes into the woods.” Lucas stopped short of the first tree and turned. “I’m not going into the woods.” Grant gave him a flat look. Lucas pointed at his feet, still firmly inside the grass. “See?” “This isn’t the time to be cute.” “I’m not being cute. I want to look.” Grant seemed ready to argue, then a shout came from the medical area and he turned away.

Owen joined him. “You always make friends this fast?” “I sell construction equipment.” “That a yes?” “It means I get paid to disagree with people without making them stop taking my calls.” Owen grinned. Together they studied the forest. The nearest trunks were enormous compared to most Texas trees Lucas was used to seeing outside parks; thick roots broke through dark soil before disappearing beneath mats of fernlike growth, and small blue flowers clustered around some of them. Something translucent buzzed past, about the size of his thumb with too many legs. Lucas leaned away. “Not a fan?” Owen asked. “I support insects conceptually.”

Something moved between two trees—a flicker of brown, low to the ground, then nothing. “You saw that?” Owen murmured. “Yep.” “Dog?” “No idea.” Owen stepped closer to the edge and Lucas grabbed his shoulder. “Remember the speech about not going into the woods?” “You agreed with that guy?” “I can dislike his delivery and still appreciate not being eaten.” Owen considered that. “Fair.”

When Lucas focused on the nearest strange flower with the intent to understand it, something responded.

Unknown Plant
F-Rank
Additional identification unavailable.

“Well,” he said. “What?” “Try looking at the flowers and thinking Identify.” Owen went silent; a second later his eyebrows rose. “No kidding.” “Unknown Plant?” “Yeah.” “F-rank?” “Yeah.” Lucas tried the tree.

Ashwood
F-Rank Flora
A mature ashwood tree. Additional identification unavailable.

“That one gave me a name,” Lucas said. “Ashwood.” “You too?” “Yeah.” “Either the System is translating information to both of us or the tree species actually speaks English.” “I’m leaning toward option one.” “Coward.” Owen laughed, but Lucas was already testing the grass.

Basin Grass
F-Rank Flora

He looked at Owen.

Owen Brooks
Human — Lv. 1
F-Rank

Owen’s eyes narrowed. “You identifying me?” “Yeah.” “What do you get?” “Name. Human. Level one. F-rank.” “Same.” Lucas looked toward Grant and got the same limited information. He looked into the forest again; nothing moved, yet the sensation that something watched from between the trees refused to leave.

A whistle sounded behind them. A middle-aged man held up a folding knife. “Anyone else have something useful?” People checked pockets. The arrival had transferred whatever they had been carrying, producing a bizarre inventory of modern civilization: pocketknives, lighters, pens, keys, a few firearms that caused immediate arguments, a hammer hanging from one man’s belt, a box cutter, and one woman with a purse containing two granola bars, a bottle of water, cosmetics, medication, and what looked like half the contents of a pharmacy receipt. Lucas checked his own pockets more carefully and found phone, wallet, keys, a pen, and a small folding utility knife on his keychain—nothing that deserved the word weapon.

Grant began organizing resources, and that went poorly. “You’re not taking my gun,” one man said. “Nobody said taking,” Grant replied. “You said centralize weapons.” “I said we need to know what we have.” “Same damn thing.” “No, it isn’t.” A taller man with thick shoulders and a tool pouch still clipped to his belt stepped between them. “Everybody can keep measuring each other’s testosterone later,” he said. “Right now we need water.” A few people laughed and the tension dropped half a notch. “We’ve got maybe a couple dozen bottles between hundreds of people. Anybody see a creek, pond, anything?” Owen raised a hand. “Terrain slopes west.” “You know that from what?” “Water goes downhill.” “That is disappointingly reasonable.” “I’m an outdoor guide.” “Better.” Grant nodded. “Take four people. Stay visible from the clearing.”

Lucas said, “Before anybody starts drinking mystery water, we should figure out whether the System can Identify it.” The taller man pointed at him. “That’s useful.” Grant frowned. “Identify?” Lucas demonstrated with a flower, and within two minutes half the clearing was staring intently at plants, rocks, shoes, each other, and their own water bottles. He tested his phone.

Personal Communication Device
Non-System Item
Functionality: Inactive

He tested his utility knife.

Folding Utility Knife
Non-System Item
Condition: Good

People were discovering other functions. A woman shouted that Notifications showed the Accession messages. Someone found that Progression displayed Race progress at zero percent. Lucas checked.

Human — Lv. 1
Progress: 0%
Class: Not Acquired
Profession: Not Acquired

He focused on Not Acquired and received: Class acquisition requires System-recognized development. Additional requirements vary. “Helpful,” Lucas muttered. “What?” Owen asked. “Apparently to get a Class, we have to do things that make us worthy of a Class.” “That sounds extremely specific.” “It’s practically a manual.” Owen checked his own. “Same.”

They spent the next half hour learning almost nothing and discovering that almost everyone found the same basic interface: no one had a Class, no one had a Profession, no one had visible Skills, and starting attributes varied only slightly. A muscular man announced he had thirteen Strength; another person claimed fourteen Perception; someone accused him of lying. Lucas’s own attributes remained all tens.

A scream came from the eastern edge of the clearing—not panic this time, but pain. People surged away from the sound. Someone shouted, “Animal!” Lucas ran, Owen beside him almost immediately. They pushed through a cluster of people. A man lay in the grass clutching his calf, blood covering his hands, while something small and brown darted toward the trees. Lucas caught only a glimpse: long body, low legs, a narrow head with an ugly hooked snout, dog-sized or smaller. A woman swung a purse at it; the creature twisted around with shocking speed and snapped at her ankle. She jumped backward screaming.

Identify.

Needle Rat
Beast — Lv. 2
F-Rank

The Needle Rat hissed, its mouth opening far wider than a rat’s should, four long teeth curving forward like ivory needles. “Back!” Lucas shouted. A man with a folding knife rushed in anyway. The Needle Rat lunged; he stabbed downward and missed. The creature hit his shin and drove him sideways. The crowd recoiled. Lucas grabbed a fallen branch and swung as the rat darted past. He missed by inches. Owen stomped and the animal veered, then vanished into the undergrowth.

The injured man was already being helped toward the nurse. Blood soaked the grass. Lucas looked at the branch in his hand, then at the tree line. More movement flickered between the trunks. Grant’s voice rose over the noise. “Form a perimeter! Anything that comes out of those woods, we hit it!” People scrambled for weapons—knives, sticks, a hammer, one man with a pistol he was already raising. Lucas kept the branch. Owen found a longer piece of ashwood and snapped the end against a rock until it splintered into a crude point.

“You know how to use one?” Owen asked. “No.” “That is not the answer I wanted.” “It’s the accurate one.” Lucas extended the point, found too much wobble, and shifted his rear hand closer to the end. Better. The System remained silent—no Skill, no notification.

The sun was lowering, the light taking on a warmer shade between the silver-edged leaves. They had no idea how long days lasted here, how cold the night would become, or what hunted after dark. A commotion rose near the western side of the clearing. Owen swore. The scouting group was coming back, running; one person had blood on his shirt, another supported a limping woman between them, and behind the trees branches shook—not at ground level, but higher. Something larger was moving through the forest.

Grant shouted for everyone to form up. Lucas’s hands tightened around the pointed stake. The branches shook again, closer. He opened Identify before he could see the creature and got nothing. Then something stepped between the ashwood trunks: a shape the size of a large wolf, long forelegs, dark bark-colored hide, pale eyes, a jaw lined with hooked teeth.

Bark Hound
Beast — Lv. 4
F-Rank

The hound lowered its head. A second shape appeared behind it, then a third. Owen whispered, “That seems bad.” Lucas shifted the crude spear point toward the closest beast. “Yeah.” The Bark Hound took one slow step forward. Across the clearing, hundreds of level-one Humans went silent. Lucas could hear the wind moving through alien leaves, Sarah telling the injured man not to move, somebody crying, and Grant counting the animals under his breath—three, then four, then five.

The nearest Bark Hound crouched. A translucent notification appeared at the edge of his vision.

Human — Lv. 1
Progress: 0%
Class: None
Profession: None

It vanished when he dismissed it. Nothing had changed. The Bark Hound sprang, and Lucas finally had something to learn.
