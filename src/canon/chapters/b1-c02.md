# Chapter 2
## Teeth in The Grass

The Bark Hound crossed twenty feet of open ground before Lucas finished inhaling. It didn’t run like a dog—no gather, no bounce, no warning coil of muscle. One second it was crouched at the tree line; the next it was bark-dark hide and pale eyes ripping straight at the loose knot of people holding sticks that wanted to be weapons.

Lucas reacted late, but his hands did not. The crude spear came down. The hound twisted in the air, not enough; the point scraped shoulder instead of chest, and that was still enough to change the angle so the animal slammed into the man beside Lucas instead of taking him clean. The man screamed as the hound landed, spun, and snapped. Lucas drove the spear with both hands. Wood hit hide, the point skidded too shallow, and the hound’s head whipped toward him—one frozen heartbeat of cracked skin, black gums, and hooked teeth angled backward like they were designed to keep whatever they caught.

Then Owen crashed into its side with his own stake. Ugly blow. Worked. The hound stumbled. “Again!” Lucas shouted, having no idea who he was yelling at—everyone, apparently. Daniel’s hammer came down from the other side and caught it behind the skull. The Bark Hound dropped, not dead; its forelegs folded while the back half twisted hard enough that a paw ripped clumps of grass out of the dirt. Lucas raised the spear. The hound looked at him, and he saw it about to move without knowing how. He jammed the point at the eye. Resistance first, then something gave. The hound convulsed. Lucas almost lost the shaft. Daniel hit it again, and the body went still.

A line of text appeared.

Meaningful combat contribution recognized.
Race progression gained.

Human — Lv. 1
Progress: 18%

Lucas stared at it for half a second. Then someone screamed behind him. The rest of the pack had arrived. The clearing came apart. Five Bark Hounds had come in; Lucas had picked one, and the others hadn’t waited their turn. One tore through the loose line twenty feet to his right and a woman went down. Another lunged at three men with lengths of wood and shoved them backward without even touching them. The crowd behind the “defenders” surged away, making it worse—people tripped, someone went over a bag, a child started screaming.

“Hold the line!” Grant yelled. There was no line, only scared people standing in a line-shaped suggestion. A Bark Hound proved the difference by slipping between two of them and heading for the center of the clearing, toward the injured, toward Sarah. Lucas ran. His first step nearly failed; his legs felt light, not stronger or faster, just full of adrenaline. He shoved past someone. The hound was already ten yards ahead. “Sarah!” She looked up from the bitten man. The hound changed direction. She had no weapon. Lucas threw the spear—a terrible throw that spun once and smacked the hound sideways across the ribs. The animal flinched, and that was enough. Sarah rolled back and hauled the injured man under the shoulders.

The hound rounded on Lucas. “Bad throw,” Owen said, breathless, from somewhere behind him. Lucas didn’t take his eyes off the animal. “Helpful.” “I specialize in feedback.” The Bark Hound growled. Lucas backed toward the fallen stake. The animal’s eyes tracked him left, adjusted; right, adjusted again—not attacking, watching. “Don’t rush it,” Owen said. “I wasn’t planning to.” “Good.” The hound sprang. Lucas dove for the spear, caught the shaft, and the animal hit him before he could get up. Pain detonated through his left shoulder. He rolled. Teeth snapped inches from his face, bringing wet earth, blood, and something woody and sour on the hound’s breath. He shoved the spear crosswise between them; jaws closed on wood and cracked the shaft. Lucas kicked wild. Boot hit chest, barely moved it. Owen hit it from the side. The hound dropped the broken stake and turned. Lucas scrambled back. Shoulder burned. He touched it—no blood, bruise or strain maybe. It had hit him with its body instead of its mouth.

A man charged from behind Owen with a tire iron and swung at the head. The animal ducked, missed, then went for his thigh. “Back!” Lucas shouted, too late. Hooked teeth tore through denim. The man screamed and went down. The hound yanked once and blood sprayed the grass. Lucas moved before he had a chance to have a better idea, broken spear in both hands as a short stabbing weapon, and drove the jagged end into the hound’s flank. This time it went in, not far, but far enough. The animal let the man go and snapped at Lucas. Owen hit the muzzle. The hound staggered. The injured man swung the tire iron from the ground and something broke; the rear leg collapsed. “Now!” Lucas yelled. Three people hit it at once—hammer, wood, metal—and the hound died under the pile.

Lucas stumbled back, breathing hard. Another notification appeared.

Human — Lv. 1
Progress: 41%

Still level one. He looked at the man on the ground, blood pulsing from the torn thigh, and progress suddenly felt like a pretty stupid thing to be thinking about. “Sarah!” Lucas shouted. She was already moving, hands on the wound, orders snapping at the people around her. “Belt. Give me a belt. Not there. Higher. Tight.” Lucas turned back toward the perimeter. Three hounds left. One dead near Grant, finished with help from Daniel and whoever else had been close enough to swing. Another driven toward the trees. The last circled the edge of the clearing, snarling at six people who’d finally worked out that standing shoulder to shoulder made them harder to pick off. The pack had lost the first advantage. People were learning, slowly and painfully, but learning. The remaining hounds seemed to notice. The one near the trees backed off.

Grant stepped forward. “Don’t chase!” A man with a bloodied shovel ignored him and ran after it. Lucas swore. The Bark Hound gave him five more steps. The man followed. The forest swallowed him to the knees. Then the hound turned, and two more shapes moved behind the trees—not Bark Hounds, smaller Needle Rats. The man stopped. “Back!” Grant shouted. He turned. The Bark Hound hit him from behind. The sound he made was short. Jaws closed on the back of his calf and dragged. He went face-first. Lucas started forward. Grant grabbed his arm. “No.” “He’s alive.” “And there are more in the trees.” The man screamed again. Lucas stared. The hound dragged him farther. Two people stepped toward the woods; Grant blocked them. “We go in, we lose more.” Lucas hated him for saying it and also knew he was right. The man clawed at the grass. One of the Needle Rats darted forward. Lucas looked away and told himself it was because another hound was still in the clearing. It wasn’t. The last Bark Hound backed toward the trees. Nobody followed. It stared at them for a few seconds, then vanished between the trunks. The screaming stopped shortly after.

No one spoke. The clearing had been noisy since the Accession—questions, arguments, crying, orders, panic—and now hundreds of people stood in near silence. Lucas stared at the place where the man had disappeared. He didn’t know his name, and that bothered him more than he wanted it to. Grant let go of his arm. “You would’ve gone after him.” Lucas looked at him. “Maybe.” “You would’ve died.” “Maybe.” Grant’s expression tightened. “I’m not interested in maybe.” Lucas almost answered, but Sarah shouted for help and the moment ended. They had living people to deal with: three serious bite wounds, at least a dozen minor injuries from the attack or from falling in the panic, one woman with a deep puncture through her forearm. The first man bitten by the Needle Rat was pale but stable. Sarah moved between patients like the rest of the world had been put on a slower setting. Lucas carried water, held pressure, tore someone’s shirt into strips. When Sarah told him to keep a wounded man awake, Lucas talked about anything—the equipment yard, traffic, the fact that their new world apparently thought rats needed knives for teeth. The man laughed once, then grimaced. “Keep doing that,” Sarah said without looking up. “Comedy?” “Talking.” “Less flattering.” “You’ll survive.”

It took nearly an hour before the immediate crisis settled. No one else died. Lucas counted that as a victory, because the other option was worse. The Bark Hound corpses stayed where they’d fallen—four of them, the fifth escaped. One human body was missing in the woods. Nobody suggested retrieving it, not yet. Grant moved the camp inward. This time nobody argued. People used clothing, bags, tools, and dragged branches to make a rough circular boundary. It wasn’t a wall, barely an inconvenience, but it gave frightened people something to do. Daniel organized work teams. “Nothing beyond sight of the clearing,” he repeated. “You see movement, you come back. I do not care how useful the branch is.” Owen helped check the western approach. Lucas went with him. His shoulder had stiffened; every time he lifted the broken spear past chest height, pain ran down his arm. Sarah had checked it. “No deformity,” she’d said. “Probably a deep bruise. Maybe mild strain.” “How long?” She gave him a look. “On Earth?” “Fair.” “Try not to get hit there again.” “Strong medical advice.” “You’d be amazed how often people ignore it.”

Now Lucas rolled the shoulder carefully while they walked. Owen noticed. “You okay?” “I’ve had better sales calls.” “That bad?” “One guy once made me sit through forty minutes of complaining about diesel emissions before telling me he needed an electric machine.” Owen stared at him. “You handle trauma strangely.” “I’m experimenting.” They stopped where the clearing narrowed toward the western tree line. The scouts had found water before they came back—that was the good news. The bad news was why they’d run. “A stream,” Owen said. “How far?” One of the returning scouts pointed. “Maybe half a mile. Downhill. We filled bottles. Then we heard something moving on the ridge above us.” “Bark Hounds?” “Couldn’t tell. Bigger than those.” Nobody liked that. The water they’d brought back Identified as:

Ashwood Stream Water
F-Rank Natural Resource
Potability: Uncertain

Lucas read it twice. “Uncertain is a cruel word.” Daniel crouched beside the bottles. “Can we boil it?” “Probably.” “Probably?” “Fire kills microbes. I have no idea whether alien mana parasites respect Earth chemistry.” Owen nodded solemnly. “That sentence improved my day.” A young woman with glasses stepped closer. “Boiling still makes sense.” Lucas looked at her—dirt on one knee, holding a plastic water bottle like it might explode. “I’m Emily,” she said. “Chemistry grad student.” Daniel pointed at the bottle. “Any wisdom?” “Not enough to make promises. But if we have no other source, boiling is better than drinking it untreated.” “Finally, someone willing to gamble with science.” Emily frowned. “That is not what I said.” Daniel grinned. Lucas liked him more every hour.

Lucas opened Progression again.

Human — Lv. 1
Progress: 41%
Class: Not Acquired
Profession: Not Acquired

Forty-one percent from two meaningful fights. He hadn’t killed either hound alone—first one redirected the charge, stabbed the eye, helped finish it; second hit it while it was busy trying to eat someone else. The System had counted contribution, exactly like the messages said. He focused on Human progression and received: Race progression reflects meaningful development across System-recognized paths and significant personal adaptation. System-recognized paths. Class and Profession, presumably. He had neither. Significant personal adaptation had to be the current progress. The Bark Hounds had taught him something, or the System thought they had. He looked at the broken spear. One lesson was not up for debate: this weapon was garbage.

Lucas found a spot near Daniel’s work group where people were sorting usable branches and picked through them—too crooked, too heavy, too short—until one piece of ashwood was nearly six feet and mostly straight. “Planning something?” Daniel asked. “Trying not to fight level-four murder dogs with landscaping scraps.” “Good goal.” Lucas held the branch horizontally. Better weight than the old stake, still rough, one end forked. He borrowed a hatchet from a man who’d arrived with it on a work belt. “Bring it back.” “If I lose it, assume something ate me.” “That is not reassuring.” Lucas chopped the fork off, then used his utility knife to strip the small branches and carve the end. Slow work. Hands hurt. Shoulder complained. Still primitive when he was done, but when he tested the balance the point didn’t wobble like it was trying to leave.

Owen wandered over. “New spear?” “Less terrible stick.” “Catchy.” Lucas shifted his grip. The first fight replayed: he’d held the spear too high, the Bark Hound attacked low, and when he dropped the point he’d caught the shoulder—not skill, geometry; the hound had to pass through the space where the point existed. That mattered. He tried again, point low, rear hand near his hip, front hand loose. Imagining the charge, his instinct was to thrust, and it felt wrong; moving the point made missing easier. What if he did less—set the point and let the animal provide the force? Lucas planted his rear foot. Owen watched. “You know what you’re doing?” “No.” “You keep saying that.” “Because it keeps being true.” Lucas repeated the motion: lower, set, brace. He thought about how the first hound had twisted when it saw the point, so showing the point too early was probably a bad idea. He raised the spear a little and dropped it only on the imagined charge. Better? Maybe. No System notification. Lucas smiled faintly. “What?” Owen asked. “Nothing.” “That looked like a smile.” “The System isn’t giving me a Skill for waving a stick around.” “And that makes you happy?” “A little.” Owen shook his head. “You are weirdly demanding of our kidnappers.”

Lucas stopped. Kidnappers. Was that what the System was? Maybe, maybe not. It had taken them, it had also warned them, given information, granted progression. Neutral, maybe. That didn’t mean he trusted it. “You check your progress?” he asked. “Human twenty-six percent.” “From the fight?” “Probably. I hit two.” Lucas nodded. “Mine’s forty-one.” Owen looked at him. “Show-off.” “I got hit.” “Ah. Premium XP package.” “Wouldn’t recommend it.”

Grant called a meeting before sunset. Not everyone came. Enough did. He stood near the center of the clearing with Daniel, Sarah, and a few others. “We need watches through the night,” Grant said. “We need water teams in the morning. Nobody leaves alone. Nobody leaves without telling someone where they’re going.” A man crossed his arms. “And you’re in charge now?” Grant exhaled. “No.” “Sounds like it.” “I’m saying what needs doing.” “According to you.” Daniel stepped between them verbally before the argument could catch. “Anybody got a better idea for making sure we don’t get eaten in our sleep?” Nobody spoke. “Excellent,” Daniel said. “Democracy survives another day.” A few people laughed. Grant looked annoyed. Daniel ignored him. “We need volunteers for watches. Four directions. Six people per position. Rotate every two hours.” Lucas raised his hand. Owen did too. Grant saw them. “You two take east first shift.” Lucas glanced at the darkening forest. “Why east?” “That’s where the first rat came from.” “Strong sales pitch.” “You volunteering or not?” “Yes.” “Then east.”

The argument over authority didn’t disappear; it just lost priority. People formed watch groups. Sarah organized a separate medical station. Emily worked with others cataloguing plants gathered near the clearing. Daniel started planning simple barricades. Lucas noticed something: Grant gave orders, Daniel built systems, and people listened to Daniel more willingly. The sun dropped. The alien sky went violet near the horizon. The cloud bands overhead caught orange for a few minutes and turned into enormous glowing roads across the heavens—beautiful, terrifying. Lucas would have appreciated it more if the forest weren’t full of teeth. He ate half a protein bar donated from someone’s work bag. His stomach complained. He drank boiled stream water after it cooled—metallic. Identify changed.

Boiled Ashwood Stream Water
F-Rank Natural Resource
Potability: Acceptable

That one word got more celebration than anything else had. Acceptable. Human civilization had fallen far enough in one afternoon that acceptable water felt luxurious. Lucas stood watch with Owen and four others. Two flashlights, both still worked. Phones didn’t. Vehicles didn’t. Simple battery-powered flashlights had transferred functional. Lucas filed that away. The rules behind what had failed weren’t obvious—System interaction, complex electronics, power sources? He didn’t know. His flashlight beam swept between trunks. Silver edges of ashwood leaves reflected faintly. “Movement,” one watcher whispered. Everyone froze. Lucas raised his spear. Something small moved. Identify.

Needle Rat — Lv. 2

It stopped at the edge of the beam, then retreated. “Do we kill it?” someone asked. Lucas almost said yes. Forty-one percent. Another fight could mean Level 2. The thought came fast, too fast, and he disliked it immediately. The rat hadn’t attacked. Chasing it into the woods at night for progression was exactly how you made corpses. “No,” Lucas said. A man named Chris frowned. “Why not?” “Because it wants us to follow.” “You know that?” “No.” “So—” “I know going into the dark after it is stupid.” Owen nodded. “Strong argument.” The rat disappeared. Twenty minutes later another appeared, then two. They circled the light but stayed outside spear range. “They’re testing us,” Chris whispered. Maybe. Or they smelled blood from the hound corpses. Lucas looked back. The bodies were still near the edge of the camp.

The rest of the watch passed in tense quiet. Near the end of their shift a larger shape moved among the trees—not a Bark Hound. Identify gave nothing until it stepped partially into the open: Mossback Ape, Beast — Lv. 7. Seven levels above them. It watched the clearing for nearly a minute, then turned and disappeared into the deeper forest without attacking. Lucas felt the weight of that decision settle on everyone present. They had been spared, or at least not chosen. When the next watch arrived, Lucas and Owen returned to the center of the camp. Sarah checked his shoulder again and told him to rest it. He sat with his back against a makeshift barrier of branches and bags, spear across his knees. His shoulder hurt. His hands were blistering. He was hungry. A man had died because they didn’t understand the forest. A Mossback Ape seven times their starting level had spared them because they hadn’t behaved like idiots. A Bark Hound had attacked because it found an opening. Rules. Patterns. Consequences.

Lucas looked at the spear. The System hadn’t granted him a Skill. It hadn’t offered a Class. But he was different from yesterday. He knew where to hold the point. He knew not to chase. He knew monsters could choose not to fight. He knew the System cared about contribution rather than killing blows. He knew Race progression could come from adaptation even before a Class. And he knew he was one meaningful fight away from Level 2. The thought should have scared him. It did. It also made him want to know what happened when the number changed. Across the clearing, a Needle Rat appeared at the edge of the trees. Lucas saw it. So did three other people. No one moved. The rat sniffed the air, then vanished. Lucas smiled faintly. Yesterday he’d arrived with nothing but questions. Now at least he knew which questions might keep him alive. A new notification appeared.

Significant adaptive behavior recognized.

Human — Lv. 1
Progress: 90%

Lucas’s smile vanished. Three percent. For not fighting? He looked toward the place the rat had disappeared, then at the spear in his hands. The System was not rewarding violence. It was rewarding development. That distinction might matter more than any of them understood. He closed the notification. Tomorrow, he intended to find out.
