# Chapter 16
## The Price of a Trail

The argument began with three cuts in a tree.

Lucas had expected something louder.

Grant had called the meeting shortly after breakfast, and by the time Lucas reached the southern gate twenty-three people had gathered around Priya’s route board. The map from yesterday had been pinned beneath a strip of hide to keep the morning breeze from turning Hearth’s new transportation network into litter. Hearth. Lowbank. Stonefield. Three settlements connected by charcoal.

Beside the board Marcus held a pale length of ashwood bark marked with three horizontal slashes. Ironbound. Priya held another piece marked with a simple arrow. Neutral. Grant stood between them.

Lucas stopped beside Owen. “Twenty-three people for a discussion about trail signs.”

Owen folded his arms. “Civilization is healing.”

“Terrifying.”

The joke earned half a smile, but Lucas could feel the tension underneath. This was not really about signs. Everyone knew that.

Grant raised his voice enough to carry. “We need repeatable routes. People are already moving between Hearth and Lowbank. Stonefield wants regular food shipments. Lowbank wants medicine. We need clear markers, maintained crossings, patrols, and rules about who uses hunting grounds along those routes.”

There it was. Hunting grounds.

Marcus planted the marked bark against the board. “If Ironbound crews clear a trail, maintain it, patrol it, and drive predators away, the trail should be marked so people know who is responsible for it.”

Priya replied, “Responsible isn’t the same as owning it.”

“No one said owning.”

“Then don’t put a faction mark on every tree.”

Marcus’s jaw tightened. Lucas understood his position more than he wanted to. Ironbound crews had been doing real work—cutting timber, maintaining watches, organizing hunting teams, clearing brush from routes that other people happily used afterward. Grant’s system produced results. Results had weight. The problem was what happened when weight became authority without anyone noticing the moment it changed.

Grant looked toward Lucas. “You mapped the route. Opinion?”

Lucas disliked being summoned into politics almost as much as he disliked bad spear technique. “At which part?”

“The markers.”

“Neutral.”

Marcus exhaled through his nose. Lucas continued before the man could respond. “Put Ironbound marks on work sites if you want. Timber stand. Maintained bridge. Patrol station. But the route between independent settlements shouldn’t look like one faction owns it.”

“Again,” Marcus said, “nobody said owns.”

“Then neutral markers cost you nothing.”

That landed. Marcus did not like it. Grant’s expression stayed controlled. “What about hunting?”

Lucas glanced at the map. That was harder. The timber route Marcus had shown them yesterday passed through grazer sign and near the northern edge of the Lowbank route. Hearth hunters used part of the same region. Freewalkers used it too. Animals did not care about administrative boundaries. Hungry humans did.

Grant pointed to a broad area southeast of Hearth. “Ironbound teams have spent four days learning these herds. We built two funnels and a stake line. Yesterday an independent hunting party pushed a grazer herd east before our scheduled hunt. We lost most of a day.”

A woman Lucas recognized as Tessa spoke from the crowd. “We didn’t know you had a scheduled hunt.”

“You passed our marks.”

“Three scratches on a tree aren’t a legal code.”

Owen murmured, “I like her.”

Grant ignored the comment. “The point is predictability. If six teams chase the same animals whenever they feel like it, we get injured people, scattered herds, and no reliable food.”

Lucas could not argue with that. So he did not. “You’re right.”

Several people looked at him. Marcus looked almost suspicious. Lucas pointed at the map. “You’re right about coordination. Not ownership.”

Grant’s eyes narrowed slightly. “Difference?”

“A schedule says your team hunts a herd this morning and other teams stay clear because interfering is stupid. Ownership says the herd is yours because you put marks on trees.”

“And if we built the funnel?” Marcus asked.

“Then the funnel is yours.”

“The herd uses the funnel.”

“The river uses Lowbank. Does that make the river theirs?”

Marcus opened his mouth. Closed it.

Owen whispered, “That was annoyingly good.”

Lucas wished it felt good. It did not. Because Grant’s problem remained real. Food could not depend on everyone improvising forever.

Grant looked around the group. “Fine. We test something.” That surprised Lucas. Grant pointed at the southeast hunting area. “Today. Ironbound has a planned grazer hunt. Lucas, Owen, Priya, and anyone else who thinks shared territory works can come. We see where the conflicts actually happen instead of arguing from a board.”

Lucas considered his thigh. Then Sarah, who stood near the back of the crowd. She saw him look. “No running.”

Grant almost smiled. Apparently that was permission.

Two hours later Lucas was walking south with eleven people—five Ironbound, four Freewalkers if Lucas accepted the label, Priya, and Daniel, because Grant wanted someone who understood structures to look at the hunting funnels. The route followed the maintained trail Lucas had used yesterday. Field Record settled known landmarks into place. Creek bridge. Split ashwood. Low ridge. Ironbound timber turn. Then they left the route. Unknown resumed. Lucas appreciated the clean boundary.

Marcus led. He moved confidently through the forest, stopping twice to inspect old grazer sign. His Warrior Class had changed him subtly over the last several days—not transformed, sharpened. He carried a heavy chopping spear with a broad head fashioned from System-grade monster bone and metal scavenged from transferred tools. Not elegant. Effective.

The first Ironbound funnel appeared between two stone shelves. Daniel whistled. “They built this?”

Marcus nodded. Cut logs narrowed a natural passage. Angled stakes discouraged animals from breaking sideways. Brush had been woven between trees to make alternate exits look less appealing. It was good work.

Lucas walked the edge. Field Record captured the location after he studied it. “Useful.”

Marcus gave him a flat look. “Thank you for the professional assessment.”

“Explorer. Common. My rates are reasonable.”

That got a reluctant snort.

The second funnel lay farther east. They never reached it. Owen stopped. “Tracks.”

Fresh grazer sign crossed the path. A lot of it. Marcus crouched. “They moved.”

Tessa asked, “Because of yesterday?”

“Maybe.”

Owen walked twenty yards east, then returned. “Something pressured them north.”

Grant, who had come despite claiming this was a field test rather than a leadership exercise, frowned. “Predator?”

“Could be.”

Lucas felt the old tightening in his thigh. Stonehide Ravager. But the Ravager’s known territory was farther north. And these tracks were wrong for panic. The grazers had moved fast, but not scattered.

Lucas crouched beside Owen. Field Record brought yesterday’s three-toed tracks to mind—large, deep, west to east, then north. He looked around. “Any three-toed sign?”

Owen’s head came up. “Good question.”

They found it five minutes later. One print in soft ground. Three broad toes. Deep. Recent. Priya swore quietly. Marcus stared at Lucas. “You’ve seen that?”

“Yesterday. Sunken Road.”

Grant’s attention sharpened. “Same creature?”

“Same track shape. That’s all I know.”

No invented certainty. Field Record did not identify it. Neither did Lucas.

Owen followed the sign twenty yards and stopped. “It’s circling south.” Toward the hunting area. The political argument suddenly felt smaller.

Grant said, “We pull back.”

Marcus looked at him. “Our hunt?”

“We don’t know what made that print.”

Lucas respected the decision immediately. Then a shout came from the east. Human. Everyone moved. Sarah’s no-running order lasted approximately six seconds. Lucas compromised with an aggressive limp.

They broke through brush into a shallow clearing. Three people stood around a dead Redhorn Grazer. Not Ironbound. One was Rachel Moore from Stonefield. Another Lucas did not know. The third held a short bow.

Marcus stopped hard. “What are you doing here?”

Rachel turned. Her expression went from relief to confusion. “Hunting.”

“This is our hunting ground.”

There it was. Not theoretical anymore. Rachel looked around at the forest. “Your what?”

Marcus pointed west. “Our funnels are half a mile from here.”

“We came from Stonefield.”

“You crossed marked territory.”

“We crossed trees.”

Lucas almost admired the symmetry. Grant stepped forward. “Everyone lower weapons.” No one had raised one. Which was probably why he said it early.

Rachel recognized Lucas. “You told Thomas the route was shared.”

“I said the route was known.”

Her eyes narrowed. Fair. Lucas continued. “And I said nothing about Ironbound owning the forest because they don’t.”

Marcus turned toward him. Grant raised one hand. “Not helping.”

“Accurate, though.”

Rachel pointed at the dead grazer. “We tracked this herd from east of the ravine. Stonefield needs meat.”

Marcus replied, “That herd is one we’ve been managing.”

“You put collars on them?”

“No. We spent four days building funnels and learning their movement.”

“And we spent all morning tracking them.”

Both were right. That was the problem.

Lucas looked at the grazer. Clean kill. Arrow behind the foreleg. Another wound at the neck. Stonefield had earned it.

Then Owen said, “Quiet.”

Everyone stopped. A branch snapped south of the clearing. Heavy. Another. Lucas sensed mana—not the sudden compressed surge of a charging grazer. Something broader. Steady. Moving. Identify found nothing through the brush.

Marcus shifted beside him. The argument vanished from his posture. Warrior now. Lucas lowered the Channelwood Spear. His thigh protested. Ignored.

A shape moved between trees. Gray-green hide. Long body. A head low to the ground. Then it stepped into view. Three toes.

Identify.

Ridgeback Stalker

Beast — Lv. 8

F-Rank

It stood waist-high at the shoulder and twice as long as a large wolf, built low and dense. Bone ridges ran from its skull down its spine. Its forelimbs were thick. Its rear legs longer. Its mouth opened sideways farther than Lucas liked.

Owen whispered, “Well. That answers one question.”

The Stalker ignored the humans. It stared at the dead grazer. Rachel tightened her grip on the bow. Lucas said, “Don’t.”

“It wants our food.”

“Yes.”

“We need that food.”

Also yes.

The Stalker advanced. Marcus stepped forward. Grant caught his shoulder. “Level eight.”

“We have twelve people.”

Lucas watched the creature’s mana—dense around the spine, not building for a charge. Yet. Iterative Insight gave him no magical answer. Only the familiar sense that he lacked enough trials to know what mattered. Wonderful.

Lucas looked at the dead grazer. Then at the humans. “This is a stupid fight.”

Rachel stared at him. “Stonefield needs meat.”

“So does Hearth.”

“Then why give it up?” Marcus asked.

“Because we’re about to fight a level-eight predator over one carcass while arguing about who owns animals.”

The irony was almost insulting. The Stalker moved closer. Its back ridges lifted. Mana changed. Lucas felt it. “Back.”

Everyone moved three steps. The Stalker stopped. Interesting. Lucas moved forward one. The ridges rose higher. He moved back. They lowered slightly. Territorial distance. Or food defense. Not enough evidence. But useful.

“Owen.”

“I see it.”

Grant made the decision. “Leave the carcass.”

Rachel’s face hardened. Grant looked at her. “I’ll replace the meat from Hearth stores.” That surprised everyone. Including Marcus. Grant continued. “You tracked it. You killed it. Losing it because our hunting areas overlap and we brought you into a predator encounter isn’t Stonefield’s problem alone.”

Rachel studied him. Then nodded once. They withdrew. The Ridgeback Stalker waited until they were fifty yards away before approaching the grazer. No chase. No fight.

Lucas’s Mana Spearman progress did not move. Good. Explorer did.

Explorer — Lv. 2

Progress: 57%

Field observation. Hazard identification. Meaningful route knowledge. The System apparently approved of not being an idiot. Lucas was beginning to like his Profession.

They regrouped at the first funnel. Nobody resumed the hunt. Instead Grant drew lines in dirt with a stick. “Hearth here. Stonefield east. Lowbank south.” He marked the Ironbound funnels. Rachel marked where her group had entered. The overlap was obvious.

Marcus stared at it. “We can’t have everyone hunting wherever they want.”

Rachel replied, “Stonefield can’t ask permission from Hearth every time we need food.”

Lucas crouched beside the map. “What if nobody asks permission?”

Grant looked at him. “Explain.”

“Shared board. Planned hunts. Not claims.”

Marcus frowned. Lucas continued. “Hearth posts where teams intend to hunt. Stonefield does the same when someone travels between settlements. Lowbank too if they start hunting this far north. If you see another team’s active area, you avoid pushing herds into them. If you build a funnel, mark the structure as yours. Not the animals.”

“And if someone ignores it?” Grant asked.

“Then they’re an ass.”

Daniel coughed. Grant waited. Lucas sighed. “Then we deal with the person. We don’t pretend trees create borders.”

Rachel nodded slowly. Marcus did not. But he was thinking.

Daniel pointed at the dirt map. “You also need maintenance credits.” Everyone looked at him. “If Ironbound builds a bridge everyone uses, pretending that labor is free will cause the same fight later. So record it. Work crews. Materials. Repairs. Settlements contribute labor or resources to shared infrastructure.”

Grant smiled faintly. “That sounds familiar.”

Daniel shrugged. “Construction always becomes accounting eventually.”

Owen looked genuinely horrified. Lucas considered the idea. Responsibility without automatic ownership. Contribution without territorial claims. Not perfect. Nothing involving humans would be. But workable.

Marcus tapped the dirt near the funnels. “And hunting structures?”

“Owned by whoever builds them,” Lucas said. “Using one without permission is different from walking through the forest nearby.”

Rachel nodded. “That I can live with.”

Marcus looked at her. “Stonefield helps replace stakes if your hunters use our funnel?”

“If we use it, yes.”

A deal. Small. Unimpressive. Probably more important than half the System notifications Lucas had received.

Grant stood. “We take it back to Hearth. Claire and Thomas need a say before anything becomes standard.” Lucas liked that too. Grant was still centralizing. But he was not stupid enough to pretend two independent settlements had become his departments overnight.

The walk back was slower. Lucas’s thigh had begun aching from the brief run. Sarah was going to notice. Sarah noticed weather changes in injuries Lucas had forgotten he possessed. Field Record incorporated the new hunting area—Ironbound funnel one, unfinished second funnel east, Stonefield approach, Ridgeback Stalker encounter site, observed response to distance around fresh kill. Not safe. Not hostile by default. Observed defensive behavior around food. That wording mattered.

At Hearth the argument resumed. But it was different now. Rachel stood beside Priya’s map. Marcus stood opposite her. Grant described the encounter without embellishment. Then Daniel proposed shared infrastructure records. Priya proposed neutral route markers with faction or settlement marks only on structures maintained by a specific group. Owen proposed that anyone who carved a personal symbol into every tree should be forced to carry Daniel’s accounting board for a week. Nobody adopted Owen’s proposal.

A provisional agreement emerged before sunset. Routes between settlements would use neutral directional marks. Work sites, bridges, hunting structures, timber stands actively being harvested, and temporary hazards could carry group marks. Hunting areas could be posted as active for a limited period, not claimed permanently. Known predator sign would be shared. Infrastructure users were expected to contribute repairs, materials, labor, or equivalent resources when practical. Nothing was law. There was no government capable of making it law. It was an agreement. For now, that was enough.

Grant caught Lucas as the crowd dispersed. “You think I’m trying to own everything.”

Lucas considered lying. “No.”

Grant waited. “I think you’re trying to make everything predictable.”

“That’s bad?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

Lucas looked at the route board. “People start confusing the person who organizes a thing with the person who owns it.”

Grant’s face went still. “And people start confusing independence with not owing anyone anything.”

Lucas nodded. “That too.”

Grant seemed mildly surprised. Lucas continued. “Freewalkers use Hearth’s medicine. Food stores. Walls. Watch lines. Some act like being independent means those appeared naturally.”

Grant folded his arms. “So you agree with me.”

“I agree with the problem.”

“Not the solution.”

“Not all of it.”

Grant gave a quiet laugh. “You’re difficult.”

“I’ve been told.”

“You also keep ending up in the middle.”

“That part is against my will.”

Grant looked toward the southern gate. “Stonefield will send two people tomorrow. Lowbank the day after. We’ll work out routes and hunting schedules.”

“Good.”

“And Ironbound will keep maintaining the timber trail.”

“Also good.”

“With our mark at the timber stand.”

Lucas smiled. “Congratulations on your trees.”

Grant walked away shaking his head.

Sarah appeared approximately twelve seconds later. Lucas considered escape. His thigh made the decision for him. “You ran.”

“A little.”

“How little?”

“Philosophically?”

She checked the wound anyway. The swelling had returned. Not catastrophic. Still enough to earn a look that suggested future lectures. Lucas accepted it. Better a lecture than a Stalker.

That night he checked Progression again.

The Profession was climbing on observation, route work, and the occasional decision to not fight a predator. He liked that. The day had mattered anyway.

Priya’s board now contained a new symbol—three short lines around a small circle. Predator hazard. Beside it she had written:

RIDGEBACK STALKER — LV.8 — OBSERVED FOOD-DEFENSIVE — DO NOT ASSUME SAFE.

Lucas approved. Below that, a second note had been added.

ACTIVE HUNT AREAS ARE SCHEDULES, NOT CLAIMS.

Someone had underlined NOT CLAIMS twice.

Marcus, passing the board, stopped beside Lucas. “Your handwriting?”

“No.”

“Priya’s?”

“Probably.”

Marcus grunted. Then he pointed at the Stalker note. “We could have killed it.”

“Maybe.”

“Twelve of us.”

“Maybe.”

“You hate that word.”

“I respect it.”

Marcus looked at him. Then smiled slightly. “Next time?”

Lucas thought about the creature’s ridged spine, the steady mana, the way it had responded to distance. “If there needs to be a next time.”

Marcus nodded. That was enough. Lucas watched him leave.

The first serious fight with Ironbound had ended without punches, blood, or a faction split. That did not make it unimportant. Hearth had crossed a line today. Not a border. A different kind. The first days in the Hollow Reach had been about whether people could survive monsters. Now they were beginning to decide how they would survive one another.

Lucas looked at the map. Roads connected people. Apparently they also gave people more efficient ways to argue. Explorer had not warned him about that. Common Profession. Typical.
