# Chapter 25
## The Price Of Glass

By breakfast, the hole beneath Stonefield had acquired three guards, two ropes, four warning signs, and an argument about ownership.

Lucas considered the guards useful.

The ropes were sensible.

The warning signs were overdue.

The ownership argument had arrived faster than expected.

He stood thirty feet back from the eastern quarry edge with a bowl of grain mash in one hand and the Channelwood Spear resting against his shoulder. His right hip felt better than it had the previous evening, which was not the same as feeling good.

Megan had already explained the distinction twice.

The first time had involved anatomy.

The second had involved threats.

Lucas had understood both.

Below the new fracture, pale Glassroot crossed the broken stone in three thick branches. Morning light reached only the upper portion of the collapse, leaving the deeper junction suspended in shadow.

Every few minutes, one of the Stonefield guards glanced down as if expecting the Broodguard to climb out and request directions.

It had not.

That was not reassuring.

Thomas Reed stood beside Priya's temporary map board with Grant Hale, Daniel, Emily, Marcus, and two people Lucas recognized from Stonefield's kiln crews. Caleb Moore leaned against a stack of unused quarry blocks nearby.

Nobody looked particularly happy.

Owen appeared beside Lucas carrying his own bowl.

“How long?”

“Since before I got here.”

“The argument.”

“I know.”

Owen looked toward Grant and Thomas.

“Any blood yet?”

“No.”

“Disappointing.”

Lucas ate another spoonful.

“Marcus is there.”

“Good point. If there had been blood, there would be more blood.”

The argument was not loud.

That made it more serious.

Thomas had a piece of charcoal in one hand. Grant had one of Priya's copied route sheets. Daniel stood between them in the social rather than physical sense, which was probably harder.

“We found it under Stonefield,” Thomas said.

Grant answered calmly. “You found the breach under Stonefield. The network extends beyond Stonefield.”

“Maybe.”

“We have two confirmed surface voids outside the quarry.”

“Connected to Glassroot. Not necessarily to this junction.”

Grant tapped the map.

“That's exactly why we need coordinated access.”

Thomas's jaw tightened.

“Coordinated with who?”

“Hearth. Lowbank when relevant. Anyone whose routes or water might sit over the same structure.”

One of the kiln workers, a woman named Denise, folded her arms.

“And when Hearth decides the quarry is too dangerous to use?”

Grant did not answer immediately.

Lucas noticed that.

So did Thomas.

“There it is,” Thomas said.

Grant's expression remained level. “If part of the quarry is undermined by an active dungeon network with a Level Nine creature moving beneath it, then yes, I might argue that part shouldn't be used.”

“You don't run Stonefield.”

“No.”

“Then don't say it like you do.”

Marcus shifted his weight.

Not threatening.

Not relaxed either.

Lucas handed his empty bowl to Owen.

“Why am I walking over there?”

“Because you're incapable of watching a bad idea from a comfortable distance.”

“That's not true.”

Owen held both bowls.

“You are literally walking toward it.”

Lucas joined the group.

Thomas looked at him.

“Tell him.”

Lucas stopped.

“No.”

Thomas blinked.

Grant almost smiled.

Lucas pointed between them.

“If you already know what I think, you don't need me. If you don't, asking me to tell him is a bad start.”

Daniel rubbed a hand over his face.

“Thank you for making this easier.”

“I'm helping.”

“You have an unusual definition.”

Priya arrived carrying another sheet of bark-paper substitute and pinned it to the board.

The map showed Stonefield, the quarry, the known dungeon breach, the secondary fissure, the two Glassroot-linked surface voids, and the newly exposed eastern junction.

None of the lines connecting them were solid.

Priya had drawn dotted marks instead.

Lucas approved.

Unknown should look unknown.

Emily crouched beside a covered wooden box on the ground.

Inside lay three thumb-sized pieces of detached Glassroot.

Thomas pointed at them.

“That's the other problem.”

Lucas looked at Emily.

“What happened?”

“Nothing yet.”

“That's rarely how people introduce a problem.”

Emily lifted one fragment with tongs.

“Yesterday's detached piece responded to concentrated mana. We stopped testing. This morning I checked the fragments Stonefield had already collected before we understood the network better.”

“And?”

“One is brighter.”

Lucas leaned closer without crossing the chalk line Emily had drawn around the box.

She was right.

The middle fragment carried a faint blue-white thread.

Not a pulse.

Not exactly.

More like residual light.

“Did anyone feed it mana?”

“No.”

“Touch it?”

“With wood tongs only.”

“Bring it near the junction?”

“No.”

Emily pointed to the lid.

“It was stored with the others overnight.”

Grant looked at Thomas.

“And people want to mine it.”

Denise answered before Thomas could.

“People want to know if it can be used.”

“That's different from mining it.”

“It becomes mining eventually.”

Emily set the fragment down.

“Maybe. Or maybe it becomes something we never bring inside a shelter.”

Thomas sighed.

“We're not idiots.”

“No,” Emily said. “Which is why we're having the conversation before someone trades a glowing root to Lowbank for fish.”

Owen arrived behind Lucas.

“Was that an actual proposal?”

“No,” Thomas said.

“Shame. ‘Mystery root for fish’ has a strong early-economy charm.”

Nobody laughed except Daniel.

Barely.

Lucas looked at the quarry.

The problem was not ownership.

Not really.

Ownership was the word people were using because it was easier than the actual problem.

The actual problem had layers.

Stonefield had discovered the breach.

Stonefield had lost Rachel and probably Evan because of it.

Stonefield's quarry sat over the network.

Hearth had supplied fighters, medical support, maps, and labor.

The Glassroot might be useful.

It might also respond to mana, move, undermine stone, signal creatures, reclaim corpses, or do something none of them had observed yet.

If it had value, people would want it.

If it had danger, people would want someone else responsible for it.

Often the same people.

Lucas opened Field Record.

EASTERN QUARRY JUNCTION — ACCESS PROBLEM

Observed value:

F-Rank mana-responsive material.

Possible structural/conduit function.

Potential crafting/research value unknown.

Observed risk:

Active network.

Moving root geometry.

Undermined stone.

Broodguard Lv.9 confirmed beneath quarry. Mana-response under at least one controlled test. Unknown relation to double pulse.

Social constraints:

Stonefield controls quarry labor/site. Network extends beyond known quarry boundary. Hearth/Stonefield expedition losses shared. Other settlements may be affected if network reaches routes/water.

Question:

Who decides access when the thing being accessed does not respect settlement boundaries?

He closed the interface.

Grant was looking at him.

“I hate when you do that,” Lucas said.

“Do what?”

“Wait for me to finish thinking.”

“Would you prefer I interrupt?”

“No.”

“Then we're both suffering.”

Thomas pointed at the exposed junction.

“What would you do?”

Lucas glanced at Daniel.

“Why are you not answering this?”

Daniel smiled without humor.

“I have. They want another answer.”

Of course they did.

Lucas rested both hands on the spear.

“Stonefield controls the quarry.”

Thomas nodded once.

Grant did not react.

Lucas continued.

“That doesn't mean Stonefield controls the dungeon.”

Thomas's nod stopped.

“The network crosses boundaries we haven't mapped. If you cut a root here and something changes under the Hearth road, that's not only Stonefield's decision anymore.”

Denise frowned.

“So Hearth gets a veto?”

“No.”

“Then who does?”

“I don't know.”

That answer irritated everyone.

Lucas considered it a good sign.

He pointed to the quarry.

“We don't know enough to build permanent rules. We know enough to build temporary ones.”

Grant folded his arms.

“Such as?”

“No harvesting live Glassroot.”

Thomas opened his mouth.

Lucas kept going.

“Detached material can be studied under containment. Any test that uses mana gets recorded and watched by more than one person. Nobody widens a void or breach without telling the other settlements that use nearby routes. Stonefield decides who enters the quarry work zone because it's Stonefield's site. Dungeon entry stays a joint expedition decision until we know whether actions below can affect people above.”

Grant's eyes narrowed slightly.

“Joint between Hearth and Stonefield?”

“And anyone else directly affected.”

“That's vague.”

“Yes.”

“Vague rules fail.”

“Overconfident rules fail too.”

Marcus exhaled through his nose.

“There he is.”

Lucas looked at him.

“What?”

“The guy who wants a system with no one in charge of it.”

“I want people in charge of the parts they're actually responsible for.”

“And when the parts overlap?”

“Then they have to talk.”

Marcus looked horrified.

“That's your solution?”

“I didn't say it was pleasant.”

Daniel laughed.

This time Thomas did too.

Grant did not, but some of the tension left his shoulders.

Priya wrote on the map board.

TEMPORARY GLASSROOT ACCESS PRACTICE

Not law.

Not ownership.

Practice.

Lucas liked the word.

It admitted they were still learning.

Thomas read the first line and nodded.

“No live harvesting until we understand the response better.”

Denise grimaced but did not object.

Grant added, “Any new surface connection gets shared.”

Priya wrote it.

Daniel said, “And structural work within fifty feet of exposed Glassroot gets checked first.”

Thomas looked at the quarry.

“Thirty.”

Daniel shook his head.

“Yesterday thirty feet of ground became a hole.”

“Forty.”

“Fifty.”

“Forty-five.”

Daniel considered.

“Fine.”

Priya wrote forty-five.

Owen leaned toward Lucas.

“History will remember this as the Great Five-Foot Compromise.”

Lucas ignored him.

The agreement lasted eleven minutes.

Then a Stonefield runner came from the kiln side of the settlement.

“Thomas!”

Everyone turned.

The runner was breathing hard.

“Two Hearth guys are at the south shed. They brought trade sacks. They want the Glassroot pieces.”

Grant closed his eyes.

Marcus said, “They're not Ironbound.”

“You don't know that,” Thomas said.

“I do, because I told every Ironbound crew not to touch the stuff.”

Grant opened his eyes.

“Who?”

“Ben Holcomb and another guy. I don't know him.”

Grant's expression changed.

Not anger.

Something colder.

“Free traders.”

Lucas had heard the phrase twice in the last week.

People moving goods between Hearth, Lowbank, and Stonefield without belonging to a formal work crew.

Useful.

Inevitable.

Apparently fast.

Thomas looked at Lucas.

Lucas shook his head.

“Don't.”

“What?”

“Make that my problem.”

“You're already walking.” Owen said.

Lucas looked down.

He was.

“Damn it.”

The south shed had once stored quarry tools.

Now it held three sacks of dried grazer meat, two clay water jars, a bundle of worked hide, and five pieces of Glassroot on a wooden bench.

Ben Holcomb stood on one side.

Caleb stood on the other.

The second trader, a narrow-faced man Lucas did not recognize, had one hand on a sack.

Nobody had drawn weapons.

That was progress.

Caleb saw Thomas first.

“I told them no.”

Ben raised both hands.

“And I said we were asking.”

“You offered two sacks of meat.”

“That's how trade works.”

Grant stepped inside.

“Ben.”

Ben's face tightened.

“Grant.”

“You heard the quarry was closed.”

“I heard the shelf was closed. These are already detached.”

Emily moved immediately to the bench.

“From where?”

Caleb answered. “Old collection. Before yesterday.”

Emily examined the pieces without touching them.

“None of these leave.”

Ben frowned.

“Why?”

“Because one changed overnight.”

That silenced him.

For three seconds.

Then the narrow-faced trader said, “Changed how?”

Emily looked at Thomas.

Thomas looked at Grant.

There it was.

Information had become part of the resource problem.

If they told everyone Glassroot responded to mana, its value increased.

If they hid it, people might experiment blindly.

Lucas hated both options.

Priya arrived at the shed doorway.

“We tell them the safety information.”

Grant looked at her.

“All of it?”

“Enough not to kill themselves.”

“That's not a measurable amount.”

“It is more measurable than secrecy.”

Ben looked between them.

“You people are making glowing rock sound like plutonium.”

Emily said, “Plutonium has the advantage of being well documented.”

Owen nodded solemnly.

“Strong endorsement for plutonium.”

Lucas walked to the bench.

One Glassroot fragment was dark.

Another held a faint line.

He did not touch either.

“Tell traders three things,” he said. “Live Glassroot isn't for sale. Detached Glassroot is under study and not cleared for trade. It has responded to mana under controlled conditions, so nobody tests it casually.”

Ben stared at him.

“Cleared by who?”

Lucas looked at Thomas.

Thomas looked annoyed.

Good.

“Stonefield,” Thomas said. “For material taken from our quarry.”

Grant added, “And Hearth will give the same warning on our routes.”

Ben looked at the sacks of meat.

“So Stonefield owns it.”

Thomas's mouth tightened.

Lucas answered first.

“Stonefield owns those pieces because Stonefield collected them.”

“That's what I said.”

“No. You asked if Stonefield owns Glassroot.”

Ben frowned.

“Difference seems academic.”

“Until someone finds it under Lowbank.”

That landed.

The narrow-faced trader slowly took his hand off the sack.

“What if I find a loose piece on a road?”

Emily answered. “Mark where. Don't put mana into it. Don't sleep next to it. Bring it somewhere people can observe it.”

“Can I sell it?”

Thomas groaned.

Lucas almost respected the persistence.

“After we know it doesn't call a Level Nine Broodguard to your bedroll.”

The trader's face changed.

Ben looked at him.

“You left that part out.”

“I didn't know that part.”

“Exactly,” Lucas said.

The trade ended.

Not because authority had won.

Because uncertainty had finally become expensive enough.

By midday, copies of the temporary Glassroot practices were headed toward Hearth and Lowbank.

Priya refused to call them rules.

Grant refused to call them suggestions.

They compromised on guidance.

Owen called them “strongly worded root opinions.”

Nobody adopted his version.

Lucas spent the afternoon doing something he preferred to politics.

Failing at a Skill.

He stood on a flat section of quarry floor well away from the eastern exclusion zone. Daniel had built a simple wooden frame with a hanging sandbag at one end and a sliding stone weight at the other.

Focused Mana Reinforcement waited beneath Lucas's skin.

The evolved Skill was easier to aim.

That did not make it easy to use.

He activated it through his right hand and into the spear shaft.

The concentration formed smoothly.

Too smoothly.

Lucas shifted the active zone toward the spearhead.

Mana moved.

The first time, it moved too far and left his grip under-supported.

The second time, he overcorrected and flooded his forearm.

The third time, Daniel released the hanging bag.

The frame jerked.

Lucas's reinforcement went to the wrong place.

The spear twisted in his hands.

He let go.

It clattered across the dirt.

Daniel nodded.

“Excellent.”

Lucas stared at him.

“You've become a terrible teacher.”

“You didn't hurt yourself.”

“Low standard.”

“Your standards are why Megan has opinions about you.”

Lucas retrieved the spear.

He could feel the new Skill's advantage.

Efficiency.

A narrow reinforced region used less mana than blanketing his body or the entire weapon.

But narrow meant choices.

Choices meant timing.

Timing meant mistakes.

Iterative Insight sharpened the pattern.

He had moved the reinforcement after the load changed.

Too late.

He needed to anticipate from evidence, not guess.

Daniel reset the bag.

“Again?”

Lucas looked at his hip.

No sharp pain.

No Mana Step.

“Again.”

They repeated the drill nine times.

On the tenth, Lucas watched Daniel's hand instead of the bag.

Release.

The frame loaded left.

Lucas shifted the reinforcement before the twist reached his wrist.

The spear stayed aligned.

Better.

Not magic.

Information.

Daniel changed the release without warning.

Lucas failed immediately.

“That's rude.”

“Combatants may not provide schedules.”

“You're enjoying this.”

“A lot.”

On the fourteenth repetition, Hazard Recognition tugged at Lucas's attention.

Not danger.

Change.

The eastern quarry guards had stopped talking.

Lucas lowered the spear.

Daniel followed his gaze.

One guard stood at the warning line with a hand raised.

The ground beneath them was still.

Then the Glassroot pulsed.

Strong.

Lucas counted.

One.

Two.

Three.

Weak pulse.

East and down.

The guards waited.

Nothing happened.

Then a third pulse appeared.

Not from the junction.

From farther south.

A thin pale flash moved beneath a line of stone near the closed quarry shed.

Emily came running.

“Did you see that?”

“Yes.”

Priya arrived behind her.

“Third pulse?”

“Separate origin,” Lucas said.

“Sure?”

“No.”

She nodded.

Good answer.

They approached only to the established safe line.

The pale flash did not repeat.

Field Record opened.

POST-COLLAPSE PULSE EVENT

Observed:

Primary strong pulse at eastern junction. Secondary weaker pulse east/down after normal interval. Third pale flash observed south of junction near old tool shed.

Unknown:

Same network branch?

Response?

Independent source?

Broodguard movement?

Material response?

Do not assume sequence equals message.

Emily looked toward the shed.

“The stored fragments.”

Everyone moved at once.

Not running.

Fast.

The box sat where they had left it.

Closed.

The middle fragment was brighter.

The other two remained dark.

Ben and the traders were gone.

Nobody had touched the box according to the Stonefield worker posted nearby.

Emily crouched outside the chalk boundary.

“That's new.”

Lucas could see a thin line of light running through the fragment.

It brightened.

Faded.

Brightened again.

Not the double-pulse timing.

Something else.

Grant arrived.

“What changed?”

Emily explained.

Thomas came thirty seconds later.

By then, the fragment was dark again.

Grant looked at the quarry.

Then the box.

Then Thomas.

“This is why I want centralized control.”

Thomas's face hardened.

“This is why I want it kept here where we can see it.”

“Those aren't opposites.”

“They become opposites when your people decide centralized means Hearth.”

Grant's jaw tightened.

Lucas felt the conversation trying to fall back into the same hole.

He planted the spear butt against the ground.

“Move the fragments.”

Both men looked at him.

“Where?” Thomas asked.

“Not Hearth.”

Grant frowned.

“Why not?”

“Because then every argument becomes about whether Hearth took Stonefield's resource.”

Thomas looked slightly less angry.

“Not the quarry either,” Lucas continued.

Now Thomas frowned.

“Why?”

“Because we just watched one possibly respond while sitting near an active network.”

Emily nodded slowly.

“Distance test.”

Lucas looked at her.

“Controlled.”

“Obviously.”

“Farther from Stonefield. No mana input. Same container. Watch whether the light changes with quarry pulses.”

Grant understood first.

“If it still responds, proximity isn't required.”

“Or the relevant range is longer than we moved it,” Emily said.

“If it stops?” Thomas asked.

“Then we learned something worth repeating before believing.”

Priya tapped the map.

“There is a bare stone rise halfway between Stonefield and the bog route. No settlement. Good sight lines. No known Glassroot void.”

Daniel said, “We can build a covered observation box there.”

Thomas looked at Grant.

Grant looked at Thomas.

Neither liked it.

Which made it promising.

“Neutral site,” Priya said.

Grant sighed.

“Fine.”

Thomas nodded.

“Fine.”

Owen whispered to Lucas, “You realize you've accidentally invented a laboratory.”

“Don't tell Emily.”

“She'll demand funding.”

“I don't know what funding is anymore.”

“Probably fish.”

The fragments were moved before sunset.

Not by Lucas.

Megan arrived specifically to prevent that.

“You have spent the day standing, arguing, and doing spear drills.”

“Controlled spear drills.”

“Your hip does not care about the adjective.”

“It should.”

“It doesn't.”

She pointed toward the shelter.

“Sit.”

Lucas sat.

This was becoming a disturbing habit.

From the shelter step, he watched Stonefield adjust around the quarry.

New rope lines.

New watch rotations.

Workers moving tools away from the eastern shelf.

People asking questions.

People hearing different answers.

Information traveled faster than maps and worse than maps.

By evening, one rumor said the Glassroot could store mana.

Another said it could summon dungeon beasts.

A third said Hearth planned to seize the quarry.

A fourth said Stonefield was hiding a fortune in magical crystal.

All four had grown from pieces of truth.

Lucas hated that more than a clean lie.

Priya sat beside him.

“I need something from you.”

“No.”

“You don't know what it is.”

“That has never protected me.”

She handed him a sheet.

At the top she had written:

GLASSROOT INFORMATION NOTICE

Known.

Unknown.

Current safety practice.

Lucas read it.

“You want me to check facts.”

“Yes.”

“Not decide policy.”

“No.”

“Not explain ownership.”

“No.”

“Not attend another meeting.”

Priya hesitated.

Lucas handed the sheet back.

“You ruined it.”

“One meeting tomorrow.”

“No.”

“Lowbank is sending Claire Morgan.”

“No.”

“Thomas asked for you.”

“No.”

“Grant asked for you.”

“Definitely no.”

Priya smiled.

“You'll be there.”

Lucas looked toward Owen.

Owen was twenty feet away and already laughing.

Traitor.

Lucas took the sheet again.

The notice was good.

It said what they had observed.

It said what they had not.

It warned against mana testing and live harvesting.

It listed the quarry exclusion zone.

It did not claim the Broodguard controlled the pulses.

It did not call Glassroot valuable.

It called it potentially useful and demonstrably hazardous.

Better.

He made two changes.

“Don't say ‘connected network’ here.”

Priya looked.

“Why?”

“We've confirmed multiple Glassroot-linked locations. We haven't physically traced all of them into one continuous network.”

“Fair.”

“And change ‘third pulse’ to ‘third observed flash.’”

“Because we don't know it was a pulse.”

“Yeah.”

She corrected both.

“Anything else?”

Lucas looked at the final line.

Report new Glassroot discoveries to Hearth or Stonefield immediately.

He tapped it.

“Lowbank will hate that.”

“Why?”

“Because it makes them report to us.”

Priya stared at the line.

Then crossed out Hearth or Stonefield.

She wrote:

Report new Glassroot discoveries to the nearest settlement and share the location through the route network.

Lucas nodded.

“Better.”

“Annoyingly.”

“That's my specialty.”

Darkness settled over Stonefield.

The eastern quarry remained quiet.

No Broodguard appeared.

No collapse followed.

At the neutral observation rise, two people watched the detached fragments through the night.

At the quarry, two others watched the junction.

At Hearth, Grant's people copied the information notice.

At Lowbank, a runner carried another copy south.

For the first time since the Accession, the settlements had something worth fighting over that none of them fully understood.

Lucas suspected that made it more dangerous than if they had understood it perfectly.

He opened his Status before sleep.

Human — Lv. 5

Progress: 65%

Mana Spearman — Lv. 7

Progress: 35%

Explorer — Lv. 5

Progress: 68%

The day had moved everything a little.

Not because he had killed something.

Because he had learned a Skill's limits, documented a new hazard pattern, helped build a workable access practice, and turned private observations into shared survival information.

He closed Status.

Focused Mana Reinforcement still felt new.

The political problem felt older than civilization.

Someone found something useful.

Someone else wanted it.

Everyone wanted safety.

Nobody wanted someone else deciding what safety meant.

Below Stonefield, the Glassroot network pulsed once.

Strong.

Lucas counted in the dark.

The second pulse came weak and distant.

East and down.

He waited for a third flash.

None came.

At least not beneath Stonefield.

Farther away, on the bare rise where the detached fragments had been moved, a watcher saw one thin blue-white line appear inside the observation box.

It lasted less than a second.

Then it went dark.
