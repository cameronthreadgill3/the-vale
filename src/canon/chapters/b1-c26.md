# Chapter 26
## What Travels

By sunrise, the most valuable thing moving between Stonefield and Lowbank was not Glassroot.

It was a rumor about Glassroot.

Lucas learned that when a boy from Stonefield arrived at the neutral observation rise carrying two water skins, a sack of flatbread, and the news that Lowbank had supposedly offered three weeks of smoked fish for every pound of blue crystal Stonefield could mine.

“No,” Priya said.

The boy blinked. “No what?”

“No offer. No price. No mining.”

“Oh.”

He considered this with the grave disappointment of someone who had just watched an entire economy disappear before breakfast.

“Then why did Jonah say it?”

Priya looked at Lucas.

Lucas was sitting on an overturned clay bucket with his Channelwood Spear across his knees. His right hip was stiff rather than painful this morning, which Megan had described as progress and Lucas had described as annoyingly vague.

“Because information gets bored when it travels,” he said.

Priya stared at him.

“What?”

“It starts decorating itself.”

The boy grinned.

Priya did not.

“Please don’t encourage the rumors to develop personalities.”

“They’re already breeding.”

That earned him half a smile.

The neutral observation rise had become considerably less neutral-looking overnight.

Yesterday it had been a bare patch of higher ground beside the Hearth-Lowbank route, chosen because it sat well away from Stonefield’s quarry, Lowbank’s river, and Hearth’s walls. Today it contained a canvas rain cover, three marked stakes, a rope perimeter, two clay boxes lined with sand, a borrowed Stonefield table, and four handwritten signs Priya had insisted on making large enough that nobody could claim not to have seen them.

DETACHED GLASSROOT UNDER OBSERVATION.

DO NOT TOUCH.

DO NOT APPLY MANA.

REPORT CHANGES.

The last sign was Lucas’s favorite because it was the only one people seemed naturally inclined to obey.

Everybody wanted to report something.

Most of it was useless.

The two detached Glassroot fragments sat in separate sand-lined clay boxes beneath the rain cover. Both were roughly forearm-length, translucent in places, mineral-hard in others, with branching internal structures that looked almost organic until you remembered they had been cut from formations running through solid rock.

Fragment One had brightened twice without deliberate mana input.

Fragment Two had done nothing interesting at all.

Lucas trusted Fragment Two more.

He opened Field Record.

NEUTRAL OBSERVATION SITE — MORNING CHECK

Fragment One:

No visible movement.

Internal brightness: low.

No contact since prior observation. Last recorded event followed quarry double pulse by estimated 11–16 seconds.

Fragment Two:

No visible change.

Controls remain inadequate.

Human timing synchronization remains approximate. Do not infer connection from sequence alone.

He closed the record.

“Still nothing?” Priya asked.

“Nothing is useful.”

“You say that because you like making lists.”

“I say that because nothing is reproducible.”

“That too.”

A cart creaked on the southern trail.

Lowbank’s delegation appeared around the bend five minutes later.

Claire Morgan walked in front with Jacob Wells beside her. Two Lowbank carriers followed with baskets of smoked fish, woven reed cord, and sealed clay jars. The fifth person was unfamiliar, a compact woman in her forties with gray beginning at her temples and a short chopping blade hanging from her belt.

Claire introduced her as Nia Patel, one of Lowbank’s best fishers and increasingly their most reliable person for anything involving river hazards.

“Not a title,” Nia said immediately.

Lucas liked her.

Thomas Reed arrived from Stonefield shortly afterward with Daniel and Caleb. Grant came from Hearth with Marcus, Emily, and two people carrying copied route sheets.

Nobody had called the gathering a council.

That seemed important.

They stood around the borrowed table anyway.

Claire looked at the clay boxes.

“So those are the crystals worth three weeks of fish per pound?”

Priya closed her eyes.

The Stonefield boy suddenly became fascinated by his water skin.

Thomas sighed. “We did not start that.”

“I know.” Claire set a rolled map on the table. “Lowbank apparently offered the fish, so I’m reasonably confident we didn’t either.”

Grant’s mouth twitched.

The humor lasted perhaps ten seconds.

Then the actual problem began.

Claire unrolled her map.

It showed the route from Lowbank toward Stonefield, copied from Priya’s earlier work, with three new marks along the northern side of the reed flats.

“These appeared yesterday afternoon.”

Lucas leaned closer.

“Sinkholes?”

“Maybe. Small ones.” Jacob pointed. “First is barely knee-deep. Second has a crack beneath it. Third we didn’t approach because the reeds around it were leaning inward.”

Lucas felt Hazard Recognition stir.

Not warning.

Recognition.

Known indicator.

Inward-leaning vegetation had preceded the shallow Glassroot-linked void north of Stonefield.

“Any light?” he asked.

“Not during daylight.”

“Pulse?”

“We weren’t watching for one.”

Claire tapped the route.

“The problem is the third depression is less than forty yards from the dry ridge people use between Lowbank and Stonefield.”

That changed the meeting.

Glassroot was no longer merely beneath Stonefield.

Maybe.

Lucas refused the word until they had evidence.

Grant said, “We need to inspect it.”

Thomas nodded once.

Claire looked at both of them. “Lowbank needs to inspect it.”

A small silence followed.

Grant corrected himself.

“We need to inspect it together.”

Better.

Thomas looked at Lucas.

Lucas disliked the direction of everyone’s eyes.

“I’m not in charge of holes.”

Marcus folded his arms. “You’re becoming weirdly specialized in them.”

“I object to that professionally.”

“You’re an Explorer.”

“Not a Hole Inspector.”

Nia laughed.

Claire did not ask Lucas to lead. Instead she asked the question he actually wanted.

“What would you need to know before deciding whether the route stays open?”

That was different.

Lucas looked at the map.

“Whether the depressions are actually Glassroot-linked. Whether they’re growing. Whether the dry ridge is undermined. Whether pulses happen there. Whether moving people or carts changes anything.”

Emily added, “And whether there’s water moving underneath. Lowbank’s channels shift.”

Daniel nodded. “If flowing water is washing out soil around something rigid, the root might be incidental.”

“Or supporting it,” Thomas said.

“Or weakening it,” Grant added.

Lucas pointed at all three of them.

“That is exactly the problem.”

Nobody knew.

Which meant the first job was not deciding who controlled Glassroot.

The first job was finding out whether the road was about to fall into it.

They left the observation rise in two groups.

Claire, Jacob, Nia, Lucas, Priya, Daniel, Emily, and Marcus went toward Lowbank’s new depressions. Grant and Thomas stayed behind long enough to establish synchronized watchers between Stonefield’s quarry and the detached fragments.

The plan was simple enough to sound reliable.

At the next quarry double pulse, Stonefield would raise a red cloth.

A runner stationed on the midpoint ridge would relay the signal with another cloth.

The observation rise would record exact visible changes in both fragments.

Lowbank’s route team would watch the depressions if they were safe enough to approach.

Human synchronization.

No magical clock.

No instant communication.

Just people, sightlines, and colored cloth.

Primitive did not mean useless.

Lucas walked rather than hurried.

His hip tolerated the first mile.

The second produced a dull pull when the ground sloped sideways.

He adjusted his stride.

Megan had cleared normal travel that morning with three conditions: no Mana Step, no running, and no pretending Focused Mana Reinforcement could substitute for healthy tissue.

Lucas had pointed out that the third condition seemed personally targeted.

Megan had agreed.

Focused Mana Reinforcement remained tempting.

That was the problem with a new Skill. It made every ordinary inconvenience look like an invitation.

Uneven ground?

Reinforce the leg.

Heavy pack?

Reinforce the shoulder.

Stiff hip?

Absolutely not.

He had already learned that strengthening a force path did not remove the consequences of sending force through injured tissue.

So he left the Skill alone while walking.

Lowbank’s reed flats smelled of wet earth and green water.

The first depression was unimpressive.

Lucas appreciated that.

Danger did not have to arrive with glowing eyes and a level marker.

A shallow bowl had formed beside the route, perhaps three feet across and eight inches deep. The soil was damp but intact.

Identify gave him nothing useful beyond ordinary mud, grass, and stone.

Daniel probed the center with a long pole.

Solid at eighteen inches.

They marked it and moved on.

The second depression was different.

A narrow crack crossed the bottom.

Lucas crouched outside the soft edge.

Hazard Recognition pulled his attention to three details.

The crack was clean along one side.

The grass roots beneath the lip were exposed.

A faint line of moisture disappeared into the fracture rather than collecting in the bowl.

Known indicators of active subsurface movement.

Not proof of Glassroot.

Daniel pushed the probe down.

Two feet.

Three.

Then the resistance vanished.

He stopped.

“Void.”

“How deep?” Claire asked.

“No idea.”

Emily knelt farther back and watched the crack.

“Any mana?”

Lucas concentrated.

The ambient mana around Lowbank was different from Stonefield’s quarry. Wetter. Diffuse. Harder to separate from the river channels.

But beneath the crack was something cleaner.

Faint.

Structured.

“Maybe.”

Marcus looked at him.

“That sounded confident.”

“It wasn’t.”

They marked the depression with three stakes and continued.

The third sat exactly where Jacob had described it.

Reeds leaned inward around a dark oval perhaps six feet across.

The dry ridge route passed thirty-eight yards south.

Lucas stopped well outside the visible depression.

“Change.”

Everyone halted.

The word had traveled faster than Glassroot rumors, which Lucas considered one of humanity’s better recent accomplishments.

“What changed?” Claire asked.

Lucas pointed.

“Ground slope.”

The depression itself was obvious.

The subtle problem was not.

The earth between the hole and the route had a shallow sag running through it.

Maybe an inch.

Maybe less.

Priya saw it after he pointed.

Daniel saw something else.

“Those reeds aren’t just leaning toward the hole.”

He traced a line east with the probe.

“They’re following the sag.”

Claire’s face tightened.

“How far?”

“Let’s find out without standing on it.”

They worked outward.

Stakes first.

Rope next.

No one crossed the suspected line.

Lucas opened Field Record and added each observed point as Priya mirrored them on paper.

The sag ran east-northeast for almost sixty yards.

Not directly toward Stonefield.

Not directly toward Lowbank.

It crossed beneath the established dry-ridge route.

That was worse in a practical sense.

Marcus looked back at the trail.

“How many people use this every day now?”

“Twenty yesterday,” Claire said. “More if trade keeps growing.”

“And carts?”

“Six.”

Daniel swore softly.

Lucas understood.

The route network had made the danger more important.

Before Chapter 15, this could have been a hole in an empty marsh.

Now it sat beneath the path carrying fish, clay, messages, medicine, labor, and rumors between settlements.

The route had not created the hazard.

It had created exposure.

“What do we do?” Jacob asked.

Claire looked at Daniel.

Daniel looked at the sag.

“Move the route today.”

“How far?”

“Until we know what’s under it? Far enough that I stop thinking about it.”

“That is not a distance.”

“It’s the most honest one I have.”

Lucas studied the ground north of them.

Field Record contained the bog-edge landmarks from his earlier travel.

Not a map of unknown territory.

But enough observed information to compare known options.

“There’s a stony rise northwest of here,” he said. “We used it coming from Stonefield. Adds maybe twenty-five minutes on foot. More for carts.”

Claire nodded. “Can it carry weight?”

“People, yes. We never tested loaded carts.”

Daniel pointed to Marcus.

“Congratulations. You’re now a cart.”

Marcus looked offended.

“I’m at least two carts.”

They tested the alternate route with people first.

Then with one lightly loaded handcart Nia had sent back to Lowbank to retrieve.

The rise held.

The approach needed brush cleared and one muddy turn reinforced with stone, but it was usable.

By midday, Lowbank runners were redirecting traffic.

Nobody voted.

Nobody claimed authority.

A hazard existed.

A workable alternative existed.

People used it.

Lucas liked systems best when they emerged that way.

Then the midpoint watcher raised a red cloth.

Priya saw it first.

“Pulse!”

Everyone turned toward Stonefield.

The distant relay cloth snapped upward on the ridge.

Lucas counted.

One.

Two.

Three.

Nothing visible happened at the depression.

Four.

Five.

Six.

A pale blue line appeared beneath the crack.

Claire inhaled sharply.

The light did not fill the hole.

It moved.

East to west beneath the sag.

One narrow line.

Then darkness.

“Time?” Lucas asked.

“Six seconds from relay,” Priya said.

“Relay delay unknown.”

“I know.”

Emily crouched outside the rope.

“That was Glassroot.”

“Probably,” Lucas said.

She pointed at him.

“Identify it when we can see it.”

The crack had widened.

Only slightly.

Enough.

Daniel backed everyone farther away.

“Route stays closed.”

Nobody argued.

The second signal came eleven seconds later.

Weaker quarry pulse.

The relay cloth rose.

Lucas counted again.

This time the depression remained dark.

Five seconds.

Ten.

Fifteen.

Nothing.

Then Nia said, “There.”

Farther west, beyond the mapped sag, a pale flash flickered beneath a stand of reeds.

Not at the depression.

Not on the route.

Thirty yards away.

A new point.

Lucas felt the familiar itch of pattern trying to become conclusion.

East/down at Stonefield.

Detached fragment response at the observation rise.

Now a delayed flash west of a Lowbank-linked void.

It would be easy to call it a network.

Too easy.

Field Record opened.

LOWBANK ROUTE EVENT — SYNCHRONIZED OBSERVATION

Known:

Quarry strong pulse signaled by relay. Approx. 6 seconds after relay signal, pale line visible beneath Depression Three sag. Second weaker quarry pulse signaled. No immediate response at Depression Three. Approx. 15+ seconds after relay, separate pale flash observed west of mapped sag.

Unknown:

Actual transmission delay from quarry. Relay delay. Whether observed lights share cause. Whether subsurface structures connect physically. Whether second flash corresponds to weak pulse.

Operational conclusion:

Route crosses confirmed mana-responsive subsurface hazard. Keep closed regardless of mechanism.

That last line mattered most.

They did not need to solve Glassroot to stop driving carts over it.

The ground gave a tiny shiver.

“Back,” Daniel said.

Everyone moved.

A section of the depression’s eastern lip slumped inward.

No dramatic collapse.

No monster.

Just wet soil folding into darkness.

The new opening exposed something translucent beneath the mud.

Lucas used Identify from distance.

GLASSROOT VEIN

F-Rank Material Formation Mana-responsive mineral-organic growth Condition: Exposed / Disturbed

Claire read his expression.

“Confirmed?”

“Yes.”

She looked toward Lowbank.

“So it’s here too.”

Thomas’s argument about Stonefield controlling its quarry remained true.

Grant’s argument that the hazard could cross settlement boundaries had just become more true.

Lucas wished reality would stop rewarding everyone simultaneously.

A sound came from the reeds.

Hooking clicks.

Marcus had his shield up before Lucas consciously identified the direction.

Hazard Recognition did not tell him monster.

It pulled together known evidence.

Freshly disturbed Glassroot.

Pulse.

Collapse.

Clicking from reeds.

Skitterers clicked.

“Move away from the opening,” Lucas said.

Nia already had her chopping blade out.

A shape pushed through the reeds.

Not a Skitterer.

A Marsh Strider.

Beast Lv.4 F-Rank.

It stepped into the open on long hind legs, narrow body twitching, hooked forelimbs raised.

Everyone held.

They had seen Striders before without aggression.

This one was agitated.

Its head kept turning toward the exposed Glassroot.

Then toward the humans.

Then back.

“Don’t crowd it,” Owen would have said if he were here.

Lucas heard the absent advice anyway.

Marcus shifted one step sideways.

The Strider flinched.

“Still,” Lucas said.

It moved toward the hole.

Stopped.

A faint pulse passed through the exposed vein.

The Strider bolted.

Not at them.

Across them.

Straight toward Nia.

She jumped back.

Her heel caught a reed root.

Lucas’s body tried to solve the distance with Mana Step.

He stopped himself.

Megan’s restriction was not optional because the moment felt important.

Instead he planted the Channelwood Spear.

Focused Mana Reinforcement activated.

The Strider’s shoulder struck the shaft.

Lucas did not reinforce everything.

He watched the load.

Spear contact.

Hands.

Left shoulder.

Back.

Left leg.

The focus moved with the force.

Not perfectly.

Too much mana reached his right forearm.

Too little supported the butt of the spear against the ground.

Iterative Insight sharpened the mismatch.

Lucas corrected before the shaft slipped.

The spear flexed.

The Strider deflected sideways rather than stopping dead.

That was enough.

Nia regained her feet.

Marcus moved between them.

The Strider sprinted into the reeds and vanished.

No one chased it.

Lucas released the Skill.

His right hand tingled.

His hip did not hurt.

Good trade.

Marcus looked at him.

“You could have killed it.”

“Maybe.”

“Didn’t need to.”

“No.”

Claire stared after the Strider.

“Was it reacting to the root?”

Lucas shook his head.

“We saw it react near the root. That’s all.”

She smiled faintly.

“I’m learning your language.”

“It’s mostly the word ‘unknown.’”

“Very versatile word.”

They withdrew another fifty yards and waited.

No Skitterers appeared.

No Broodguard emerged from the hole.

No further collapse occurred.

The lack of drama felt almost suspicious.

By midafternoon they returned to the neutral observation rise.

Grant and Thomas were already there.

So were two traders who had apparently heard that Lowbank had discovered a Glassroot mine worth six weeks of fish per pound.

Priya made a noise that belonged to a much older and more exhausted civilization.

Claire handled it.

“There is no mine. There is no price. The Lowbank-Stonefield route is temporarily diverted because a subsurface hazard has been confirmed near the old path.”

One trader asked, “But it is Glassroot?”

“Yes.”

“So there’s more of it.”

“Yes.”

“Then it’s valuable.”

Claire looked at Lucas.

Lucas shook his head.

“Existing in two places is not a valuation method.”

The trader frowned.

“It means there’s supply.”

Emily answered before Lucas could.

“It means there’s material. We still don’t know whether carrying it around is safe.”

That quieted him more effectively.

Priya updated the public notice.

KNOWN:

Glassroot confirmed beneath a route hazard west of Stonefield and near Lowbank travel corridor. Detached fragments have shown unexplained brightness changes under observation. Live Glassroot has responded around known pulse events.

UNKNOWN:

Whether all observed Glassroot is physically connected. Whether detached fragments remain connected in any functional sense. Whether pulses are signals, resource movement, structural response, or something else. Whether Glassroot is safe to harvest, trade, store, or transport.

CURRENT SAFETY PRACTICE:

No live harvesting.

No unsupervised mana testing. Report new exposed formations or subsurface voids. Divert travel around undermined ground.

Grant read it twice.

“This changes the access discussion.”

Thomas’s jaw tightened.

“It changes the hazard map.”

“It proves the effects aren’t confined to your quarry.”

“It proves Glassroot exists near Lowbank.”

Claire stepped between the argument before it could gather speed.

“And it proves neither of you owns the problem.”

Silence.

Lucas decided Claire Morgan was terrifying in a very efficient way.

She continued.

“Stonefield controls Stonefield’s quarry. Lowbank controls whether people use Lowbank’s routes. Hearth controls Hearth’s work crews. Information that affects the routes between us gets shared.”

Grant nodded first.

Thomas took longer.

Then he nodded too.

No constitution appeared.

No one declared a federation.

They simply had one more practical rule because the world had produced one more practical problem.

The synchronized observation results were worse for certainty and better for safety.

At the quarry, the strong pulse had occurred first.

At the neutral rise, Fragment One brightened after the midpoint relay but before the second quarry signal reached the site.

At Lowbank, the exposed vein lit after the strong signal.

The later western flash occurred after the weak signal.

The timings did not line up cleanly enough to prove anything.

They did line up often enough that nobody wanted to dismiss them.

Lucas added all three observation chains to Field Record.

He marked the delays as estimates.

He marked every relay point.

He marked the possibility that observers were simply missing faint events.

Then he wrote the part that mattered.

Information now travels faster than proof.

Priya looked over his shoulder.

“That sounds philosophical.”

“It’s a warning.”

“It can be both.”

The traders left carrying no Glassroot.

They did carry copies of the safety notice.

Lucas suspected the notice would mutate before sunset.

At least this time the rumor would have competition.

Daniel found him near the variable-load frame before evening.

“You’re limping less.”

“Megan said normal walking is fine.”

“She say anything about this?”

Daniel tapped the frame.

“Stationary practice is fine if it doesn’t hurt.”

“That sounds dangerously close to permission.”

Lucas gripped the horizontal bar.

Daniel shifted the hanging stone weight.

The load changed.

Focused Mana Reinforcement moved through Lucas’s hands, forearms, shoulders, and back.

He felt the mistake before the frame twisted.

Too late again.

He reset.

Second attempt.

Daniel changed the weight earlier.

Lucas watched the rope angle.

Not the load itself.

The cue before the load.

He moved the focus as the rope tightened.

Better.

Third attempt.

Different direction.

Lucas missed it.

The bar jerked.

He released.

“Still bad,” Daniel said.

“Less bad.”

“Your standards are inspiring.”

Lucas smiled.

That was the actual lesson.

Focused Mana Reinforcement did not make him faster at reacting after something happened.

It rewarded understanding what would happen next from evidence already present.

Rope angle.

Footing.

Weapon position.

Enemy movement.

Load path.

Not prediction.

Preparation.

He opened his Status once the practice ended.

Human — Lv.5

Progress: 67%

Mana Spearman — Lv.7

Progress: 43%

Explorer — Lv.5

Progress: 84%

No level.

No new Skill.

Good.

Today had not been about killing something large enough to make numbers jump.

It had been about discovering that the map was changing underneath the roads people had only just begun trusting.

At dusk, the three settlements sent runners along those roads.

Stonefield sent quarry closure information toward Hearth and Lowbank.

Lowbank sent the detour route and Glassroot hazard marks east and north.

Hearth sent copies of the Known / Unknown / Current Safety Practice notice to its hunting crews and timber teams.

Someone carried smoked fish toward Stonefield.

Someone else carried fired clay pots toward Lowbank.

A third runner carried a message asking whether Hearth’s healer could look at a child with a fever.

The route network moved all of it.

Food.

Warnings.

Trade.

Fear.

Help.

Bad information.

Better information chasing it.

Lucas stood at the edge of the observation rise as the last light faded.

Fragment One sat beneath the canvas.

For almost an hour it did nothing.

Then the distant quarry signal rose.

Red cloth.

Strong pulse.

The midpoint relay answered.

Lucas counted.

Fragment One brightened.

Not all at once.

A single internal line lit from one broken end toward the center.

Then stopped.

Priya recorded the time.

Emily watched without touching it.

They waited for the second signal.

The weak pulse came.

Red cloth again.

Relay.

Nothing happened in Fragment One.

Ten seconds.

Twenty.

Still nothing.

Then a runner appeared on the Lowbank road.

He was moving fast.

Too fast for ordinary news.

Claire stepped forward before he reached the rope perimeter.

“What happened?”

The runner bent over, hands on his knees.

“New hole.”

He dragged in air.

“West of the detour.”

Lucas felt Hazard Recognition gather around the words because the evidence now existed for them to matter.

“How far west?” Claire asked.

“Maybe half a mile.”

“Glassroot?”

The runner looked at Lucas.

“Blue light.”

Lucas did not ask whether it was connected.

Not yet.

He looked down at the route map instead.

Stonefield sat east.

Lowbank sat southwest.

The neutral observation rise lay between them.

The known Glassroot hazard had now appeared beside one route.

A second possible exposure sat half a mile farther west.

The route network Lucas had helped build was becoming something else.

Not merely roads between settlements.

A way to watch the Hollow Reach change.

He opened Field Record.

NEW REPORT — UNVERIFIED

Possible Glassroot-linked void west of Lowbank detour. Blue light reported. Requires direct observation.

Then, beneath it, he added one more line.

What travels matters as much as where the road goes.

He closed the interface.

“Tomorrow?” Priya asked.

Lucas looked at his hip.

Then at the darkening western road.

“No Mana Step,” he said.

Claire raised an eyebrow.

“That wasn’t the question.”

“It’s part of the answer.”

He lifted the Channelwood Spear from the table.

Tomorrow they would go west.

Not because Glassroot was valuable.

Not because Hearth wanted control of it.

Not because Stonefield wanted to keep it.

Not because Lowbank feared it beneath their roads.

They would go because the world had changed under a path people depended on.

And somebody needed to look before the rumor arrived first.
