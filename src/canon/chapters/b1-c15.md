# Chapter 15
## Lines Between Fires

Field Record did not draw a map, and Lucas counted that among its better qualities.

The morning after picking up Explorer he stood beside Priya’s board at Hearth, studying the charcoal lines she’d scratched across scraped planks. Hearth. Western watch line. Sunken Road. Three-Line Waystation. A stream fork south. Two hunting areas. Three spots where somebody had written QUESTION? in handwriting that got more irritated with every question mark. The map was ugly. Useful things usually were.

He opened Field Record. No glowing overlay. No floating icons. No helpful alerts about monsters behind trees or treasure chests three miles west. The route from Hearth to the Sunken Road simply settled into memory with unusual clarity—not as a picture, but as connected experience. The ashwood with the lightning-split trunk. The shallow creek crossing. The silver fern patch around the low stone ridge. The western watch mark. The buried road edge. The intact marker. Everything he’d actually seen. Nothing else.

Lucas looked away from the board. “Still works.”

Priya sat on the ground sharpening a charcoal stick against flat stone. “Congratulations.”

“I’m testing.”

“You’ve tested it six times.”

“Seven.”

“That is worse.”

Owen walked up carrying dried grazer meat wrapped in cloth. “Is he staring at roads again?”

Priya nodded. “Professionally now.”

Owen handed Lucas the food. “Living the dream.”

Lucas ate. Explorer had handed him exactly two automatic Attribute points on acquisition—Endurance and Perception. One free point had stacked with his remaining four, leaving five available. He still hadn’t spent any of them. Acquire power, refuse to use some of it, think too much. Owen claimed that was Lucas’s true Class.

Grant approached the board with a folded piece of bark in one hand. “New job.”

Lucas looked at him. “You say that like employment law survived the Accession.”

“You still want dinner tonight?”

“Excellent management philosophy.”

Grant laid the bark on the board. Two crude marks sat southeast of Hearth. “One of the southern scouting teams made contact with another survivor group yesterday.”

Priya sat up. “How many?”

“About sixty.”

“Stable?”

“Stable enough to send six people north looking for other humans.”

Owen leaned over the board. “Where?”

Grant pointed. “Near a split river channel. They’re calling the place Lowbank.”

Lucas eyed the distance. “Two hours?”

“Three, maybe. Their scouts took longer because they were searching.” Grant tapped another mark farther east. “And this came from them.” Another survivor group.

“Name?”

“Stonefield.”

“How many?”

“Lowbank says around forty, maybe fifty.”

Lucas looked at Grant. “You want contact.”

“I want a route.”

That made sense. A camp existing was information. A safe path between camps was infrastructure.

Daniel joined them, wiping wood dust from his hands. Grant kept going. “Lowbank has fish and water. Stonefield apparently has exposed rock, clay, and people who know how to make basic kilns.”

Emily arrived behind Daniel in time to catch the last part. “Clay?”

Grant nodded. Her expression shifted. “Useful.”

Daniel said, “Very.”

Hearth had hide, timber, some bone, decent hunting, limited herbs, and increasingly organized labor. Clay meant containers. Containers meant better storage, better cooking, potentially medicine, maybe eventually bricks. A road between groups had stopped being interesting because people wanted company. It was logistics.

Lucas felt Explorer react to the idea—not progression, just interest. Or maybe that was just him.

Grant pointed at the southern route. “Priya knows part of this. Owen knows the stream fork. Lowbank scouts are waiting at the southern marker.”

“And you want me to map the connection.”

“You have the Profession.”

Lucas frowned. Grant noticed. “What?”

“Explorer doesn’t make me a mapmaker.”

“No. Priya does that.” Priya smiled without looking up.

Grant continued. “You remember routes now.”

“Routes I personally observe.”

“Exactly.”

That distinction pleased Lucas. Grant was learning the Skill’s limits instead of assuming System equaled magic answers.

“Who’s going?”

“Owen. Priya. You. Two Ironbound.”

Lucas looked at him. “Why Ironbound?”

Grant’s disciplined work and combat crews had picked up the name over the last several days. Nobody seemed sure who’d started it—probably somebody complaining. Grant hadn’t discouraged it. The Ironbound weren’t a separate settlement. Not yet. They were the people who liked schedules, assigned equipment, organized teams, standard watch rotations, and Grant’s increasingly formal structure. Freewalkers were the opposite—hunters, scouts, people who left Hearth in small groups and came back with information, meat, injuries, or some combination. Lucas had somehow become a Freewalker without applying. He disliked being categorized. That probably confirmed the category.

Grant said, “Because if this turns into regular trade, my crews will maintain part of the route.”

There it was. Ownership hiding inside maintenance. Lucas stored the thought. Not a fight. Not today.

“Who?”

“Eric and Ben Carter.”

Lucas knew Eric—shovel fighter turned Common Warrior, solid. Ben was newer to him, a broad man in his forties who’d worked warehouse logistics before the Accession and now seemed happiest when carrying something heavy for a purpose.

“Fine.”

Grant nodded. “Leave in twenty.”

Sarah intercepted Lucas eleven minutes later. “You’re going where?”

“South.”

“That is not a distance.”

“Three hours.”

“That is.”

She checked his thigh. The Ravager wound had improved significantly. The two Vitality points hadn’t turned him into Wolverine, which Lucas considered healthy for the setting. The wound still pulled under long strides, but the deep soreness had eased. Swelling showed up later. His shoulder was almost normal. Ribs mostly complained only when he twisted hard.

Sarah pressed around the healing wound. “Pain?”

“One.”

“After yesterday?”

“Two.”

“You can go.”

Lucas blinked. “This is becoming too easy.”

“You want me to change my mind?”

“No.”

“No running.”

“Fine.”

“No fighting if you can avoid it.”

“Fine.”

“No underground structures.”

“That seems oddly specific.”

Sarah looked at him. Lucas nodded. “Fair.”

They left Hearth under clear morning light. Priya led for the first hour. The route south started on familiar ground—grazer tracks, old hunting stakes, a stream crossing Lucas remembered from Chapter Six. Field Record accepted the known landmarks cleanly. Then they passed the farthest point Lucas had personally traveled south. The Skill simply stopped. Nothing beyond the last known point. No map filling itself in. No directional tug. Nothing.

Lucas smiled. Owen saw it. “You’re happy because your Skill failed.”

“It didn’t fail.”

“It knows nothing.”

“Exactly.”

“That is a strange thing to celebrate.”

Lucas looked ahead at the unknown forest. “Means I still have a job.”

The terrain changed gradually. Ashwoods mixed with tall pale-trunked trees whose leaves hung in narrow strips. The ground grew damp. Fern gave way to knee-high reeds in the low places. Owen slowed. “Watch your footing.”

Lucas saw why thirty seconds later. The grass hid narrow channels cut through dark soil—not deep, but deep enough to twist an ankle. Priya marked the first. Lucas deliberately studied the pattern: water channel, soft edges, reed density slightly higher where the ground dipped. Field Record caught the observation once he consciously connected it to the route. Not “hazard nearby.” Observed sign. That was better.

They adjusted east. A direct line to the southern marker would’ve crossed more wet ground. Priya chose a low ridge instead—longer, drier. Eric asked, “Wouldn’t it be faster straight through?”

Owen pointed at the mud. “If your goal is arriving with both knees.”

Eric nodded. No argument.

They continued. An hour later they found the first human sign—a strip of red cloth tied around a branch. Priya stopped. “Not ours.”

Ben looked around. “Lowbank?”

“Probably.”

Lucas examined it. Ordinary cloth. No System significance. Below it, two boot prints pointed south. Recent. Owen crouched. “Two people. Maybe three. Came through this morning.”

They followed. The route descended. Water grew louder. Then the forest opened. A river split around a long gravel island—not a giant river, but wide enough to matter. One branch moved fast over stone. The other spread shallow across mud and reeds. On a rise above the split stood a barricade of sharpened logs. Lowbank.

The settlement looked different from Hearth right away. Hearth had grown outward from a clearing. Lowbank had grown around the river. Fish racks stood near the water. Long narrow shelters followed the bank. Boats didn’t exist yet, but several people were hollowing logs with fire and stone tools in a way that suggested optimism.

A watchman saw them. Five minutes later a woman in her late thirties approached with two armed companions. “Grant’s group?”

Priya answered. “Hearth.”

The woman looked at all of them. “Same thing?”

Lucas nearly laughed. Grant would hate how complicated that question was becoming. Priya said, “Hearth.” Good answer.

The woman introduced herself as Claire Morgan—former high school principal, current person everyone apparently listened to because disaster enjoyed irony. Lowbank had fifty-eight people. Two injured badly. One Healer Initiate. Three Rangers. Several fishers who’d discovered that System-recognized fish were still annoyingly fish-shaped.

Claire showed them the settlement. No ceremony. No speeches. Practical people had better things to do. Lowbank’s strength was obvious: water, fish, clay-like mud, reeds. Its weakness was equally obvious: flooding. The entire settlement sat above the current river level, but only barely. Daniel would’ve hated it.

Lucas studied the bank. Debris lines marked old high water—not ancient, recent enough that leaves remained tangled above waist height. “Does the river rise?”

Claire followed his gaze. “Hasn’t since we got here.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

She smiled. “No. We don’t know.”

Priya joined them. Lucas pointed at the debris. “Water’s been higher.”

Claire’s expression changed. “Storm?”

“Could be.”

Field Record stored the location and observation. Not prediction. Observed flood evidence.

Claire called over a man named Jacob Wells who handled their scouting. Jacob showed them the route east toward Stonefield. “About two hours if you stay north of the bog.”

“Bog?” Owen asked.

Jacob pointed. “Looks like meadow. Isn’t.”

That sounded promising in the worst way.

They ate with Lowbank before leaving—fish roasted over coals, no seasoning, still excellent. Claire wanted regular contact with Hearth, medicine especially. Sarah’s reputation had traveled faster than Lucas. In exchange Lowbank could provide fish, reeds, and eventually smoked meat if they built better preservation racks.

Lucas watched the conversation form around resources. No currency. No contracts. Just needs. But trade had already begun in everyone’s head. Routes mattered.

They left Lowbank with Jacob joining them to Stonefield. The eastern path followed the river for half a mile before turning inland. Jacob’s “bog” appeared forty minutes later. It looked harmless—low grass, scattered flowers, flat open ground. Owen tossed a stick. It landed. Nothing happened. He tossed a larger branch. The branch sank halfway.

“Excellent.”

Jacob said, “One of ours went in to the waist.”

“Monster?”

“No. Mud.”

Lucas considered that. The Hollow Reach didn’t need teeth to kill people.

They followed the northern edge. Field Record tracked the safe route only as Lucas personally observed it—dry hummock, bent willow-like shrub, black rock outcrop, stay upslope until the dead tree, then angle east. Priya made physical marks, not too many. A route covered in human signs could become useful to things that hunted humans. That thought had entered everyone’s planning recently.

Halfway around the bog, Owen raised a fist. Stop. Everyone froze. Movement on the far side. Three animals stepped from the reeds.

Identify.

Marsh Strider

Beast — Lv. 3

F-Rank

Tall, narrow-bodied creatures with long hind legs and short hooked forelimbs. They watched the humans. The humans watched back. No charge. No mana buildup. Lucas kept the spear low.

Jacob whispered, “Seen them twice. They don’t bother us.”

“Then we return the favor,” Lucas said.

Eric shifted his grip—not eager, ready. Good distinction. The Striders moved away. No combat. Lucas felt no disappointment. Explorer progression didn’t need blood. That was becoming important.

At the eastern edge of the bog they found a second hazard—a shallow ravine cut across the route, twenty feet wide, ten deep, water trickling at the bottom. Jacob’s people had been climbing down and up. Slow. Dangerous when carrying loads.

Ben looked at the slope. “We can fix this.”

Priya glanced at him. “How?”

“Not today. Cut steps. Rope line. Maybe bridge later.”

Ironbound thinking. Infrastructure. Lucas looked at the ravine. A route hazard became a work project. Useful. He marked both crossing points in Field Record—the existing steep descent and a narrower section forty yards north where a future bridge might make sense. Not “bridge location.” Potential crossing. Observed width. Banks. Nearby trees. Again, the Skill held what he gave it.

They reached Stonefield in late afternoon. The name came from a broad area of exposed gray rock where the forest thinned—stone shelves, clay banks, a shallow quarry already forming where survivors had begun prying loose slabs. Forty-six people. Their leader, if the word applied, was Thomas Reed. No relation to Marcus. Former mason. Which explained almost everything.

Stonefield had walls. Not good walls. Better than anyone else’s. Low dry-stacked stone lines reinforced with timber. Kilns sat near the clay pit. One already worked. Daniel would’ve fallen in love. Ben nearly did.

Thomas showed them fired bowls—crude, heavy, beautiful. Emily would’ve stolen all of them. Lucas handled one.

Identify.

Low-Fired Clay Vessel

Ordinary Crafted Item

Functional. Fragile.

He smiled. No rank. No magic. Still valuable.

Stonefield needed food. Their hunting was poor. The exposed terrain attracted fewer grazers. Water came from a spring that was reliable but small. They had clay, rock, and builders. Lowbank had food and water. Hearth had hunters, medicine, timber, and organization. Lucas could almost see the network—not a System network. People. Needs connected by paths.

Priya spread her map. Thomas studied it. “You came from Hearth to Lowbank, then here?”

“Yes.”

“Safe?”

Priya shrugged. “Safe is generous.”

Lucas pointed at the route. “Known.” That was better. He explained the wet channels south of Hearth, the ridge detour, Lowbank flood evidence, the bog edge, Marsh Striders, ravine crossing. Thomas listened.

Then asked, “Can you mark a direct route back to Hearth?”

Lucas looked northwest. A triangle—Hearth, Lowbank, Stonefield. The direct line from Stonefield to Hearth might be shorter. Might also cross something terrible. Field Record offered nothing. Unknown.

Lucas smiled. “Not today.”

Thomas frowned. “Why not?”

“Because I haven’t walked it.” He explained the Skill. Thomas seemed disappointed. People liked magic that removed uncertainty. Lucas increasingly preferred magic that made uncertainty obvious.

They camped at Stonefield. Sarah had not authorized overnight travel. She also had not explicitly forbidden it. Lucas decided not to think about that sentence too hard. The alternative was walking six hours back in fading light, which would be medically stupid. He chose the interpretation Sarah would hate less.

Stonefield gave them a shelter floor. Lucas’s thigh swelled by evening—less than earlier expeditions, still present. He checked Progression.

Human — Lv. 3

Progress: 72%

Explorer — Lv. 1

Progress: 68%

Mana Spearman — Lv. 4

Progress: 8%

Sixty-eight percent Explorer progress. No combat. Routes. Hazards. Settlement contact. Useful documentation. Profession XP behaved exactly differently from Class XP. Good.

Lucas opened Field Record before sleep. Hearth to Lowbank. Lowbank to Stonefield. The routes were clear. Not perfect. He remembered where he had actually walked, where Priya had led, where Jacob had corrected direction, where the bog forced them north. He tried to imagine a direct Stonefield-Hearth route. Nothing. Unknown remained unknown. Perfect.

The next morning they did not take the same route back. That was Owen’s idea. “Profession test.”

Lucas looked at him. “You’ve become a bad influence in a new way.”

Thomas knew of a hunting trail northwest that eventually reached a creek flowing toward Hearth’s region—not a full route, a possibility. They took it. Jacob returned to Lowbank. Thomas sent one scout named Rachel Moore with them.

The northwest trail began well—dry ground, clear animal paths. Then it climbed through broken stone. The terrain slowed Ben. Lucas’s leg handled it better than expected. Vitality thirteen helped recovery. Endurance eleven helped sustained travel. Explorer’s Perception fifteen helped him notice small differences. But none of those solved the trail. People did. Rachel knew the first hour. Owen read tracks after that. Priya kept direction. Lucas recorded.

Three miles in they found human-cut marks. Not theirs. Three horizontal slashes on a pale trunk. Eric touched one. “Ironbound.”

Rachel frowned. “Your people?”

“Grant’s crews,” Lucas said. “They’ve been clearing timber routes.”

The marks continued. A cleared path appeared. Brush cut back. Trees marked. One crude plank crossing over a creek. Efficient. Also obvious. Ben looked pleased. Priya looked less so. Lucas understood both. A maintained route was safer for people. It was also a claim. Grant had not declared territory. But repeated labor had a way of becoming ownership in human minds. Chapter Sixteen waited inside that problem. For now, Lucas recorded.

The Ironbound route connected to Stonefield’s northwest trail within an hour’s walk. That reduced the return dramatically. They reached Hearth before midday. Grant met them at the southern gate. Marcus had arrived ten minutes ahead. Of course he had. Grant already had the expression—the one Owen called administrative predation.

“You found two groups.”

“Lowbank and Stonefield.”

“Resources?”

“Fish, reeds, water, clay, stone, basic fired pottery.”

Daniel appeared as if summoned by the word pottery. Lucas continued. “Route hazards: wet channels south, flood exposure at Lowbank, bog east of Lowbank, ravine crossing, Marsh Striders non-hostile so far. Stonefield has a northwest hunting trail that intersects an Ironbound timber route.”

Grant looked at Marcus. Marcus nodded. Grant’s eyes moved to Priya’s board. “We can connect all three.”

“Probably.”

Grant smiled. Lucas raised a finger. “Probably.” The smile shrank. Good.

Priya spent the afternoon turning Field Record and human memory into a map people other than Lucas could use. That mattered. Lucas could recall the route. Nobody else could access his Skill. So they built something shareable—landmarks, distances in walking time, hazard notes, water, terrain, places not to camp. Priya used symbols simple enough to copy. Daniel added ravine notes. Ben described potential trail work. Owen marked likely animal corridors. Rachel had given them Stonefield’s spring location and hunting boundary. Lucas checked each item against memory. Field Record made errors easier to catch—not because it knew truth, but because his observed route was organized enough to compare.

At sunset the System chimed.

Ding!

Explorer — Lv. 2

Perception +1

Endurance +1

Free Profession Attribute Points Gained: 1

Lucas opened Status.

Strength: 11

Agility: 15

Vitality: 13

Endurance: 12

Perception: 16

Intelligence: 13

Wisdom: 14

Willpower: 10

Available Attribute Points: 6

He left them unspent. Again. Owen would have something to say about that later.

The routes rested clearly in memory. Hearth to Lowbank. Lowbank to Stonefield. Stonefield to the Ironbound timber trail and back to Hearth. Useful. Incomplete. Human. Tomorrow, apparently, the map would become political.

Lucas closed the interface. Explorer had given him a profession. It had not given him an escape from people.

That evening Lucas walked the southern edge of Hearth alone—not far. Sarah’s rules still existed, and he had learned the dangerous habit of respecting them. He stopped where the first section of Priya’s new route map had been copied onto a board near the gate. Three charcoal lines. Three settlement names. Hazard marks. Travel times. Water. No ownership symbols. Not yet.

Lucas opened Field Record and compared memory to the public map. The Skill held more detail. The map held less. That was not a flaw. Priya had stripped out information people did not need. A traveler heading for Lowbank did not need to know exactly where Lucas had stopped to adjust his bandage. They did need the ridge that avoided the wet channels. They needed the safe side of the bog. They needed the ravine. They needed to know that Marsh Striders had been non-aggressive when observed, not that they were harmless. That distinction took three words on the board.

OBSERVED NON-HOSTILE.

Lucas smiled. Good. No certainty they had not earned.

A young man from Hearth stood nearby studying the map. “Stonefield really has bowls?”

“Apparently that is our standard for civilization now.”

The man laughed. “My cup is bark.”

“Then yes. They are technological gods.”

He pointed at the southern route. “Safe to go?”

Lucas looked at the word safe. Then at the hazard marks. “Safer if you go with people who know it.”

The man nodded. That answer mattered. Field Record could make Lucas harder to lose. It could not make everyone else him. So the route network needed scouts, markers, shared maps, agreements, maintenance—all the boring pieces that turned knowledge into infrastructure. The System rewarded the discovery. People made it useful.

Lucas looked once more at the three connected settlements. Then at the western line toward the Sunken Road. Ancient builders had solved this problem before humanity arrived. Roads. Markers. Waystations. Maybe rules about who maintained what. Maybe arguments about them too. Tomorrow Hearth would begin having its own version of that conversation. Lucas suspected stone monsters would be easier.
